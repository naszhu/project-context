Test Position Effects
 Test position effects are important results that help us understand features used to probe memory, and help us augment the bREM model.These test position effects are shown in Figure 2.1.b. CRs were considerably higher than hits (as is often found, and as is predicted by bREM). Hs increased strongly over tests. CRs decreased  less strongly over tests. Overall performance increased slightly over tests. Because overall performance almost remains constant, much of  the increase in Hs and decrease in CRs may be due to a shift in decision criterion as lists continue. These results are important in part because they show the opposite of output interference, and in part because they suggest a distortion of retrieval at the start of testing following each of the initial lists (see later discussion in the modeling section). 
For statistical analysis, test position effects during initial testing were examined to assess whether output interference accumulates during the test phase itself, and whether such interference differs for target recognition versus foil rejection.[^2]
Overall patterns by test position. For TO pictures, a strong negative linear trend emerged, b = -40.96, SE = 2.58, z = -15.87, p < .001, 95% CI [-46.02, -35.90], indicating that correct rejection rates declined systematically and substantially across test positions. A modest positive quadratic trend also emerged for TO pictures, b = 9.05, SE = 3.52, z = 2.57, p = .010, 95% CI [2.15, 15.94], indicating a slight inverted U-shaped pattern superimposed on the linear decline. 
Picture type effects. ST pictures showed substantially lower overall recognition accuracy than TO pictures, b = -0.89, SE = 0.04, z = -24.18, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.96, -0.82]. The estimated marginal means were 3.07 on the logit scale for foils (corresponding to approximately 95.6% accuracy) versus 2.24 for ST (approximately 90.4% accuracy), replicating the consistent finding that foil rejection is more accurate than target recognition..
Two-way Interactions: picture Type Modulation of Test Position Effects. The Test Position × picture Type interaction showed sharply diverging patterns for ST and TO pictures. The linear interaction was large and positive, b = 76.70, SE = 2.84, z = 27.02, p < .001, 95% CI [71.13, 82.26], indicating opposite linear trends—foil rejection declined (b = -40.96) while target recognition increased (b = 35.74). Average performance across test positions. To assess performance changes across test positions, we ran two analyses. Averaging across picture types on the logit scale showed no significant overall trends, linear: b = -2.61, SE = 2.48, 95% CI [-7.47, 2.25]; quadratic: b = -4.91, SE = 3.02, 95% CI [-10.84, 1.01]. These null effects reflected opposing picture-type trends (TO: b = -40.96; ST: b = 35.74).
When picture types were collapsed, overall accuracy rose from 88.3% (positions 1–5) to 90.2% (9–12) and plateaued near 89.8% (16–20). The model showed a positive linear trend, b = 10.19, SE = 2.79, z = 3.66, p < .001, and a negative quadratic trend, b = -11.42, SE = 2.19, z = -5.22, p < .001, indicating an inverted U-shaped pattern.
Thus, overall accuracy improved modestly (~2%), but this gain reflected opposing effects: ST improved while TO declined.

Study Position Effects 

Figure 4.1.a.b: shows that study position shows little effect of initial study position. This result is notably different from the serial study position effects observed during study of the initial lists. This result suggests that the initial list study position effects were not due to changes in trace strength, or they would have appeared in final testing. Forgetting did take place between initial lists and final testing, but it is hard to imagine forgetting that would only affect the primacy and recency portions of the study lists.

Final Test Results by Final Test Order and Initial List Order
Note that the backward and forward results are mirrors of each other measured by input list position or final test position. Thus in Figure 5, the first and second columns of row 1 show the same data reversed, and the first and second columns of row 2 show the same data. 

Figure 5.1.a shows output interference (OI) for all picture types. The final test order manipulation (forward, backward, random) showed effects important for modeling.  A] Initial targets have the highest final Hs, suggesting they were stored strongly; there is little OI for these pictures, possibly due to ceiling effects. B] Pictures studied only and pictures tested only had much lower Hs and differed little from each other suggesting they had been stored much more weakly than targets (i.e studied and tested pictures) but were stored with similar strengths. C] The backward condition showed strong recency, and strong OI compared to the other conditions. It is assumed that this is due to two factors acting in concert: 1] use of enough changing context in final test retrieval to produce recency – better performance for the most recent lists seen; 2] output interference (OI) that causes performance to decrease as testing continues (see the modeling section for a discussion of the factors causing OI). D] the backward condition also showed some primacy. The best account is a gradual reconstruction of changing context as the testing moves to earlier lists. This idea will be used to explain the forward data. E] The forward condition results show greatly reduced performance as lists continue. Our best account is reconstruction of changing context. E.g. When the participants are told that the targets are coming from the first list, they manage to reconstruct context that is more similar to that stored originally than the current context. This better context continues to be used as testing continues. F] The random condition provides feedback that tells the participant what list a target came from, but that list randomly jumps among lists as testing continues. It is unclear under these circumstances whether any reconstruction of context would occur. Thus in the random condition it is assumed that the context used at the start of final testing is that after the change from initial lists to final testing. (Looking ahead to the modeling, it should be mentioned that an account for the large output interference observed, as seen most unambiguously in the random condition, requires changes in context regularly as final testing proceeds).

Results of Experiment 2
199 participants completed the experiment through online recruitment via Prolific.
Data quality screening was conducted using multiple exclusion criteria following standard practices in cognitive psychology research (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). At the participant level, individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) below-chance performance (accuracy < 50%), indicating random responding; (b) overall task accuracy more than 2.5 standard deviations below the sample mean; (c) median response time more than 3 standard deviations from the sample mean, indicating atypical response patterns; or (d) more than 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, indicating insufficient processing time or rushed responding. At the trial level, responses faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were excluded from all analyses, consistent with established response window parameters.
13 participants (6.5%) were excluded based on participant-level criteria. Specifically, nine participants exhibited below-chance performance (M = 0.48, SD = 0.01, range = 0.48–0.50), indicating they were responding randomly or not engaging meaningfully with the task. Two additional participants exhibited extremely low accuracy (22.9% and 29.1%, both >3 SD below the mean) combined with below-chance performance. Three participants were excluded due to extreme response time outliers (median RTs of 281 ms, 1,654 ms, and 1,669 ms; |z| > 3.3), with one also exhibiting rushed responding (19.5% of responses < 200 ms). One participant who had been previously identified during initial screening (59.9% accuracy) was also excluded. At the trial level, 479 trials (0.31% of all test trials) were excluded due to response times outside the 150–3,500 ms window (355 trials < 150 ms, 124 trials > 3,500 ms).
The final sample consisted of 186 participants contributing 156,214 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated for their time in accordance with institutional review board guidelines.
Recognition accuracy was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial error distribution and logit link, implemented in R with the lme4 package (Bates, Mächler, Bolker, & Walker, 2015). Seven analyses examined serial position effects across experimental phases: (a) initial test—study position, test position, and between-list effects; and (b) final test—within-study position, within-test position, between-list effects by final order, and between-list effects by initial order. Fixed effects included linear and quadratic position terms, fully interacting with picture type. Random intercepts for participants were included. Position terms were entered as orthogonal polynomials to reduce multicollinearity, and significance was evaluated at α = .05. picture-type-specific trends were estimated with the emmeans package (Lenth, 2023).
Note: Experiment 2 does not vary test order in the fashion of Experiment 1. Thus the inferences made about the explanations for the results for the forward, and backward conditions are no longer relevant. Testing in Experiment 2 is in random order, and the inferences and model assumptions will be described accordingly.

2] Within-List Test Position Effects During Initial Testing
Figure 7.1.b shows test position effects conditioned on the various types of test pictures. The solid lines show current list target Hs and current list foil CRs. These show roughly the pattern of Experiment 1, with Hs rising and CRs falling as tests continue. The explanation for these results from both studies will be presented in the modeling presentation.
The most interesting results are in the dashed lines that show correct rejections (CRs) for current list foils that had been seen on the prior trial (termed ‘confusing foils’). Due to their presence on the prior trial these test pictures are highly familiar and as a result are given many false alarms (FAs), or equivalently are given low CRs. The most familiar pictures are those studied and tested on the prior trial (as demonstrated by final test results to be shown shortly). If familiarity (REM odds, or some other measure of degree of familiarity) were used to respond, these pictures would be given the greatest false alarms (FAs), and equivalently the lowest CRs. Yet these very familiar pictures produce CRs higher than pictures from the prior trial that were studied only, and not much lower than CRs for foils seen on the prior trial. Such results suggest that participants faced with highly familiar pictures that are not from the current list start using a recall process: They recall a trace likely to be one produced by the test picture and assess the trace contents. When they find information in that trace that identifies the origin of the picture producing the trace to be the prior list, they ignore the high familiarity and respond new. This is a process known as ‘recall to reject’ (e.g. Rotello, Macmillan & Tassel, 2000). This process is described in detail in the modeling section.

Figure 11 shows a very large range of individual differences in Exp. 2, similar to those shown for Exp. 1,  In contrast to Exp. 1, the data for the first ten lists exhibits quite low accuracy, and there is not a sharp decrease to the final testing level. These results are due to the very high false alarm rates for the confusing foils in Exp 2 during the testing of the first ten lists.
We repeat the reasons why these results are important for modeling: All the data predicted are an average of individual data, and hence a mixture of widely varying results. Under these circumstances, and because the data per condition is too sparse to allow separate modeling of individuals, we cannot expect precise quantitative predictions. Thus the goal of modeling is qualitative: We will produce a model that can predict the trends in the data, and will try to choose parameter values so that the predictions are not too discrepant quantitatively.
