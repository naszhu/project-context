## Slide 2: Introduction - The Recognition Problem

**Let me start with a fundamental question:** What is recognition memory?

**At its core, recognition memory** is the process of judging a current event as "familiar" or "seen before." We do this constantly in our daily lives—recognizing faces, words, objects, places. This judgment relies on multiple **cognitive** processes, including familiarity, recall, and crucially, context.

**The Core Challenge: Context**

**Here's the problem that makes recognition memory so interesting and challenging. Imagine I show you the word "TABLE" and ask, "Did you see this word on the list you just studied?" How do you know? You've seen the word "TABLE" thousands of times in your life—in books, on signs, in conversation. So how do you distinguish this particular instance from all those other times?**

**The answer is** Context—the background information that binds an event to a specific time and place. **Context is what allows you to say, "Yes, I saw TABLE on *that* list, in *this* room, about 10 minutes ago," rather than just "Yes, I've seen the word TABLE before."**

---

## Slide 3: Defining Content vs. Context

**Let me be more precise about what I mean by content and context, because this distinction is central to my thesis.**

**First, Content—the "What."** This is the specific information that distinguishes one event from another. For example, the word "TABLE"—its letters, its shape, its meaning. This is what distinguishes it from "BOOK" on the same list.

**In contrast, Context is the "When & Where."** It's the background information surrounding an event when it was stored. This includes things like the room you were in, the computer screen, your mood, and critically for my experiments, which list it was on.

**Now, why does Context matter so much?** It is essential for focusing retrieval on the specific memory episode of interest **Without context, you'd recognize "TABLE" as familiar, but you wouldn't know if it was from the study list or from your breakfast conversation this morning. This is the recognition problem I'm trying to solve.**

## Slide 4: The Modeling Framework - Basic REM

**To study these processes systematically,** I **have** built a model starting with the simplest form of the Retrieving Effectively from Memory model (REM) of Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997, **termed** bREM. 

**Why start with bREM? Because despite its simplicity, it has a remarkable ability to explain the major phenomena of recognition studies—things like list length effects, list strength effects, and more.**

---

## Slide 5: Why bREM?



**So let me explain how bREM works.**

bREM is a familiarity-based model.
It assumes decisions are based on a "global match" process.
**Here's how it works in simplified terms:**

**When you see** a test item **"TABLE", it's** matched against all memory traces in parallel.
This **parallel matching** process produces a single "familiarity" value **which can be thought of as the odds that the item is old versus new.**

**Then** a decision is made: If **the** Familiarity **exceeds a criterion threshold, you** respond "Old." **If it falls below that threshold, you respond "New."**

bREM is very simple (3 parameters) but successfully predicts many classic findings (list length, list strength, etc.).

---

## Slide 6: The Gap - What's Missing from bREM?

**However,** bREM is powerful, but its simplicity is a limitation. It's missing key processes that humans use:

**First,** Context vs. Content: bREM treats all features the same.**It doesn't have a special mechanism for context features that bind an event to a particular time and place.**

**Second,** Storage at Test: The model doesn't account for the act of testing itself creating a new memory trace. **When you're tested on an item, does that test experience get stored? bREM says no.**

**Third,** Trace Strengthening: It doesn't fully model how recalling a trace (during study or test) might strengthen or change it **in memory.**

**Fourth,** Recall-to-Reject: bREM has no way to say "This is very familiar, but I know it's from the wrong list, so I'll respond 'New'." **That requires a recall process that goes beyond simple familiarity.**

**So, my goal:** Design experiments **that would** isolate these missing processes and **provide the data needed to** build an Augmented REM model **that includes them**.

## Slide 7: Experiment 1 - Design

**Let me describe the first experiment, which was designed to investigate context change, storage at test, and trace strengthening.**


**In Phase 1, Participants went through 10 study-test cycles. In each cycle, they would:**

**First, in the Study phase:** View 20 pictures.
**Then, a Distractor phase:** Arithmetic task **to clear short-term memory.**
**Finally, the Test phase:** Judge 20 pictures.
10 Targets: From the current list**, and** 10 Foils: Completely new pictures.

**This process creates three types of "seen" items for later testing:** ST items—which were Studied & Tested as a target; SO items—Studied Only, meaning they were in the study list but not selected for the test; and TO items—Tested Only, which were foils during the initial test.

**This design allows us to compare the memory strength of items that were studied, tested, or both.**

---

## Slide 8: Experiment 1 - The Final "Test-All" Phase

**Phase 2: Final Recognition Test**

**After completing all 10 study-test cycles, participants faced** a single, long, continuous **recognition** test of 420 pictures.

**The final targets—210 items—included all pictures ever seen in Phase 1:** all the ST items, all the SO items, and all the TO items. **The final foils—another 210 items—were completely new pictures.**


**Here's the key manipulation: It was a between-subjects design.** The Forward Group was told "Targets are from List 1," and they were tested on all pictures from List 1, then told "Targets are from List 2," and tested on all from List 2, and so on through all 10 lists. The Backward Group went in reverse order—starting with "Targets are from List 10," then List 9, working backward. And the Random Group saw all targets and foils from all lists mixed randomly, with no information about list of origin.

**The question I'm asking is: Does knowing the original context—which specific list an item came from—help retrieval?**

## Slide 9: Experiment 1 - Key Results from Initial Lists

**Before I dive in, here's a roadmap: you'll see four sets of findings—initial test results between lists and within lists, then final test results between lists and within lists. This gives us a complete picture.**

**So, let's first look at the results from those 10 initial study-test cycles, We found two really important patterns, one *within* the lists and one *between* the lists.**

**Here is a really surprising finding from within list initial test results, we looked at what happens *within* a single test block. We found that** Hits (ST): *Increased* from the start to the end of the test. **While at the very same time,** Correct Rejections (TO): *Decreased* from the start to the end of the test.
**This is a big deal because the** Interpretation: This is the *opposite* of **what you'd expect from** simple Output Interference. **This finding** suggests a **much more** complex process **is at play,** like a **strategic** criterion shift or **some kind of** trace dynamics, **is** happening *during* the test **block itself.**

1. Between-List Effects

**"Alright, let's look at the second key finding from the initial tests, the between-list effects.** **The data shows a very clear pattern:** Hits (ST): Accuracy decreased across the 10 lists. **So, participants got steadily worse at identifying true targets as the experiment went on.** **At the same time,** Correct Rejections (TO): Remained high and stable. **Their ability to reject brand new items didn't change.**"

**"This specific pattern** is known as Output Interference (OI). **The** Interpretation **for this is that the** Traces from prior lists interfere, making new targets harder to identify. **In other words, the 'output' from all the previous lists builds up, creating interference... or 'noise'... that makes it harder to pinpoint the targets from the current list."**

## Slide 12: The Next Problem - Familiarity vs. Recall

**So** Experiment 1 Takeaway: Context and storage-at-test are critical. The ST **greater than** SO result **clearly** shows that retrieval — **<span style="color:#1976D2;">the act of testing</span>** — strengthens memory.

**But here's** The Problem: In Exp 1, familiarity and recall are correlated. A strong trace (like ST) is both more familiar and easier to recall. **I can't separate these processes.**

So, how can we separate them **<span style="color:#1976D2;">to see which process contributes more?</span>**

**I** need a situation where high familiarity leads to the wrong answer.
This **would** force **participants** to rely on pure recall to override **their sense of** familiarity.

**This is exactly what Experiment 2 was designed to do.**

## Slide 13: Experiment 2 - Design

**Experiment 2 was designed to isolate what I call "Recall-to-Reject."**


**Similar to Experiment 1, participants** **studied** **and were tested on 10 lists. But here's the crucial manipulation: I changed the composition of each test list.**



**Let me walk you through this figure to show you how the design works.** Each row represents one list, with the study phase on the left and the test phase on the right. In the study phase—shown in green and blue—participants see SO items that are studied only, and ST items that are both studied and tested.

**Now here's the key difference from Experiment 1:** In the test phase, the targets—shown in blue—are from the current list. But notice the purple blocks in the second half of the test: these are the confusing foils from the previous list. The arrows show you how items from List n-1 reappear as foils in List n's test.

**These confusing foils came in three types:** ST items from List n-1 that were studied and tested; SO items from List n-1 that were only studied; and TO items that were foils on List n-1. So participants are seeing highly familiar items from just moments ago, but they need to reject them because they're from the wrong list.

## Slide 14: Experiment 2 - The Dilemma

**<span style="color:#1976D2;">This situation with the confusing foils creates a dilemma that the original bREM model can't really explain:</span>**

A "confusing foil"—<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">like</span> an ST from List n-1—is HIGHLY FAMILIAR. <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">If you rely on</span> familiarity alone (<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">as in</span> a bREM process), <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">you'd be inclined to say</span> "Old," <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">leading to</span> a false alarm.

**But** the task requires a "New" response **because the item wasn't on the current list.**

To be correct, you must use recall: "I remember this **item**... but I remember it from the last list. Therefore, the answer for this list is 'New'."

**This is** recall-to-reject: using recollection of the specific context to override a **strong sense of familiarity.**

## Slide 15: Experiment 2 - Key Results (Recall-to-Reject)

**<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">The results provided clear evidence for recall-to-reject.</span>**

**First,** those confusing foils <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">led to</span> a lot of mistakes.

**As expected,** **participants** made many false alarms to the confusing foils. This confirms they were **indeed** highly familiar.

**Second,** <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">let's look at</span> the Recall-to-Reject Signature.

**Here's the key result:** <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">You might expect</span> that the most familiar items—<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">the confusing foil</span> ST(n-1)—<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">would cause the highest rate of</span> false alarms. <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">But surprisingly, that wasn't the case.</span> 

Participants actually made fewer mistakes on these highly familiar, strong ST(n-1) items compared to the less familiar SO(n-1) items. In other words, they were better at correctly rejecting the most familiar lures.

**Why?** **Because the** stronger ST(n-1) trace was easier to recall. This easier recall allowed them to retrieve its context **—<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">Oh, this was from</span> (List n-1)**"—** and successfully reject it.

**Third,** 3. Learning to Reject. 

Across the 10 lists, **I saw clear evidence of learning.** Correct Rejections for confusing foils improved **over lists. But at the same time, Hits for true targets decreased.**

**The Interpretation:** Participants learned the strategic trade-off. They started using recall-to-reject more <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">aggressively</span>, which helped them reject familiar foils but sometimes led them to accidentally reject true targets <span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">as well</span>.

## Slide 15a: Experiment 2 Results – Final Test (Between-List)

**Now let’s dive into the final recognition test results, looking specifically at how memory performed *between* lists.**

**<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">First, let’s talk about Massive Output Interference.</span>**
As participants moved through the **492-trial final test**, I saw a clear, steady decline in accuracy for *all* item types—targets and foils—across the test.
**This closely mirrors what I found for the Random group in Experiment 1**, and it provides strong evidence that *output interference*—where retrieving many items in a row makes each subsequent retrieval harder—is a general and robust principle in recognition memory.

**<span style="color:#1976D2; font-weight:bold;">Second, looking at Exposure Effects and Recency.</span>**  
Performance on the final test wasn’t random: it followed two clear patterns.

- **Exposure:** Participants remembered items better if they’d seen them more times. *The order, from best to worst memory accuracy, was:*  
  **ST<sub>n-1</sub> (twice-studied, most recent)** > **ST (same-list target, once-studied)** > **SO (old same-object)** > **TO (totally old, least exposure)**.  
  This means retrieval and repeated exposure really strengthen memory traces.

- **Recency:** There was also a **modest recency effect.** Items studied on the most recent lists (8, 9, 10) were remembered a little better than those studied in the middle lists. *You can see this in Figure 10b.*  
  This suggests that while output interference has a strong, steady impact throughout the final test, recently encountered information still has a slight benefit.

**Together, these results show that both the amount of exposure and how recently an item was studied shape memory performance—even in the face of massive output interference during a challenging recognition test.**

## Slide 17: The Solution - An Augmented REM Model

**So how do I explain these complex results? The answer is an Augmented REM model that extends bREM to include all the processes I've been discussing.**

The results of Exp 1 & 2 are very complex. I explain them with an Augmented REM model that includes:

**First,** Context Features:

**First,** The model now has separate features for Content (T) and Context (Changing/Unchanging).
Context acts as a filter to **help the model** find the right traces.

**Second,** Context Change:

**Second, I model** Context **as something that** "drifts" between lists, from study-to-test, and during the test block **itself. This context drift explains the interference effects I observed**.

**Third,** Storage of Test Traces:

**Third,** Every test, (target or foil), creates a new **memory** trace. (Explains why TO ≈ SO).

**Fourth,** Trace Strengthening:

**Fourth, when a trace is successfully recalled during test, it gets strengthened by adding/correcting features. (Explains why ST >> SO).**

**And fifth,** Recall-to-Reject:

**Finally,** A parallel recall process is added. If a trace is **strongly** recalled **and its context is retrieved**, **the model checks whether that context matches the current task context. If it doesn't match—like an ST(n-1) item being tested on list n—the model responds "New," even if familiarity is high. (Explains Exp 2).**

## Slide 18: Modeling Discussion - Assessment and Key Issues

**Let me address some important points about the modeling.**

**First,** Qualitative, Not Quantitative: The model's goal is to predict the patterns and trends **in** the data, not the exact numbers. The large individual differences **(seen** in Figs 6 & 11 in the thesis**—**) make precise **quantitative** fitting impractical. **But the model does an excellent job of capturing the qualitative patterns.**

**Second,** Auxiliary Assumptions: **To fit the complex data, I did need some auxiliary assumptions beyond the core mechanisms I just described.**

Criterion Change: The decision criterion (familiarity threshold) was not fixed. It was assumed to change during the initial tests of Exp 1 to explain the rising Hits and falling CRs **within each test list.**

Context Distortion: A temporary "distortion" of context at the start of each test was needed to explain **the** poor performance on the first few test items.

**Third,** Causal vs. Descriptive: **Finally, I want to emphasize that** The **Augmented** REM **model** is a causal, mechanistic model. Unlike standard Signal Detection Theory (SDT) which describes data with distributions, REM explains why those distributions have the shape they do. **It provides a process-level account of how memories are stored, retrieved, and used in recognition decisions.**

## Slide 19: Summary and Conclusion

**Let me conclude by summarizing the main message of this thesis.**

Recognition **memory** is not a single, simple process. **It's** a complex interaction of **multiple cognitive mechanisms:**

**First,** Familiarity: A rapid, global-matching "feeling of oldness."

**Second,** Contextual Retrieval: Using "when" and "where" **information** to find the right memory **and distinguish it from other similar memories**.

**Third,** Storage & Strengthening: Retrieval is not passive**. The** act of testing changes memory, creating new traces and strengthening **old** ones **that are successfully recalled**.

**And fourth,** Strategic Recall: **In some situations, people** use active recall to check the content and context of a memory **allowing them** to override a misleading feeling of familiarity **(Recall-to-Reject)**.

These experiments were designed to "break" simple models **like basic REM—to push them to their limits and reveal what's missing.** **The data I collected** provide**d** the **foundation for building a more complete, mechanistic model of human recognition** memory.

**The Augmented REM model represents a significant advance in our theoretical understanding. It maintains the computational and mathematical rigor of the original REM framework while incorporating the sophisticated processes that humans actually use when making recognition decisions.**
