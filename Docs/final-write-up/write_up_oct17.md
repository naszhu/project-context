Abstract
Recognition memory judgments rely on multiple interacting processes—familiarity, recall, and contextual reinstatement. These processes can be quite complex but many of the characteristic findings from laboratory studies can be described well by signal detection theory (SDT) and can be explained by just a few causal mechanisms assuming decisions are based on the familiarity of a test item. This is demonstrated by the predictions of the three parameter Retrieving Effectively from Memory model (REM; Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). Two studies use novel designs to explore a few of the most important processes that REM (and familiarity based models) omit. The studies present lists of pictures followed by recognition tests, and then use end of session recognition tests in which every picture encountered in the earlier lists becomes a target to be judged ‘old’. This manipulation explores context storage and retrieval. Experiment 1 uses three orders of final testing during which the participant is told which list contains the targets, a manipulation assessing ability to use context to aid retrieval. Experiment 2 used many foils (pictures not studied on the current list that should be given a ‘new’ response) that were encountered on the prior list, making them highly familiar. This manipulation assesses the use of recall to reject highly familiar foils. The main goal of the studies was the formulation of an extended recognition model instantiated in the REM framework. The cognitive processes in the extended recognition model incorporate the roles of context features that help identify memory traces that were stored in a given situation (e.g. in a given list), delineate context features of different types that change at different rates and at different times, lay out the results caused by storage of study and test traces in memory, show how strengthening of traces sampled during recognition testing are critical for understanding recognition, and demonstrate the use of trace recall during recognition testing to reject highly familiar pictures.
Introduction
Recognition is a process fundamental to humans, allowing us to judge that a current event is similar to one or more past events.The goal of this research is the formulation of a model of the most important processes that are used when recognition memory decisions are made for long-term event memories (also termed episodic memories). With this in mind, the studies modeled use a distracting task after list study and prior to test to clear memory traces held in short-term memory.  The research uses data from two studies with novel designs to test recognition memory. The model to explain that data (and also to explain results from other studies) is based on the ‘Retrieving Effectively from Memory (REM) model (Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997, 1998). The 1997 paper presented a simple version of the model followed by more complex extensions. The simplest version will be termed bREM (standing for basic REM). bREM is a very simple three parameter model that has proved enormously successful in predicting the major trends in recognition studies, even when using a fixed set of parameter values for all studies and conditions (see Mohamed, Meyer-Grant, & Shiffrin, submitted). Nonetheless, bREM is known to be missing quite a few processes that are important for recognizing, and the present research is designed to fill in some of the important gaps.
We begin by outlining the core mechanisms of bREM. bREM is an example of the most commonly used account of recognition by familiarity. Familiarity is almost always assumed to be produced by global matching processes (see discussion in Gillund & Shiffrin, 1984). Global matching is a term used to describe retrieval that uses similarity to match the test item to all memory traces in parallel, combining the comparisons to produce a ‘feeling of familiarity’ that is used to decide whether the test item is old or new. This type of recognition modeling is distinct from recognition decisions based on recall or retrieval of individual traces (an important component of present modeling).
bREM is also a variant of the most commonly used way of analyzing and modeling recognition studies: signal detection theory (SDT; Green & Swets, 1966; Rotello, 2017). As described below, both bREM and SDT posit distributions of decision variable ‘strengths’. The strengths are used to produce a decision and response, either ‘old’ (an item studied, or ‘target’) or ‘new’ (an item not studied, or ‘foil’).  Both approaches have a distribution of strengths for foils and another for targets, with higher variance for the target distribution. Both have a decision criterion such that strengths below the criterion produce a new response and strengths above produce an old response. The differences lie in the shapes of the distributions and the fact that the decision criterion and the parameters defining the distributions are largely constant in bREM and are for the most part estimated freely in SDT. The distributions in SDT are assumed to be Gaussian, whereas the distributions in bREM are enormously skewed toward large values, especially for targets. 
We aim to produce an extended model based on bREM rather than SDT because bREM and its extension are causal mechanistic models positing the basis for the strengths and the decision criterion that are used to decide. 
Discussing bREM allows us to show how a few critical processes are sufficient to account for a surprising number of observed trends in recognition studies. It also allows us to set the stage for the later elaborations of the model that are layered on top of bREM—including the use of context, the dynamics of context change, differentiation of feature types, the storage of new traces at test, the updating of existing traces at test, and the contribution of recall to recognition decisions.This discussion of recognition processes missing from bREM leads naturally to the rationale for using REM as the basis for formulating a (more) general account of recognition memory. The missing processes also motivate the design of the two new studies that are presented in the next section. bREM was developed for words, but the present studies use pictures, so the following discussion uses the term pictures in place of words.
bREM:
A] Representations of Pictures and Traces
                Every picture and every memory trace of a picture is represented by a vector of feature values (arbitrarily set to 20 by Shiffrin & Steyvers,1997). For each picture, each value is chosen independently from a geometric distribution with values 1, 2, etc. The values represent base rate frequencies of that feature in the environment of the experimental context, or both. 1 represents the most common value of that feature and higher numbers are successively less frequent. The decrease in values is geometric with parameter g (set to 0.4 in REM 1997 and 0.3 in the present research, though these values make little difference in the pattern of predictions for the present studies). 
B] Storage of Memory Traces 
Let us assume N pictures are studied. Study produces a distinct memory trace for each picture. When an picture is studied each of its feature values is stored with probability u or not stored at all (the number 0 is used to represent a missing value in the trace). If stored the value is stored correctly (i.e. copied into the trace) with probability c; with probability 1- c the value stored is a random choice from the geometric distribution. In REM 1997 u was set to (the equivalent of) 0.4 and the present modeling uses 0.4 as well (though again this value is not critical for producing the patterns of data in the present studies). REM 1997 was used to predict studies using words as stimuli, and set c = 0.7. The present studies used pictures, and pictures of the type used in these studies and most others (common objects taken from the set used and studied by Brady Konkle Alvarez and Oliva (2008) are remembered better than words. Thus in the present modeling c for pictures was set to 0.85 for Experiment 1; it was set to a lower value of 0.7 for Experiment 2 (for reasons inherent in the design of Experiment 2; basically the confusing foils used in that study cause generally poorer memory). The result of storage is N incomplete and partially correct memory traces. 
C] Recognition Decisions in bREM 
For the studies modeled in this research (and in most other research) recognition testing uses 50% targets and 50% foils, important for allowing use of a decision criterion that will produce almost the same number of old and new responses.
When a list of N words is studied and a word is tested, the 20 values of the test word features are matched in parallel to the twenty incomplete and error prone values in each of the N traces. Each feature comparison produces a likelihood ratio that the value in the trace was produced by study of the test picture vs study of something else; matching values produce likelihood ratios greater than 1 and mismatching values produce likelihood ratios less than 1. When a trace value is missing (value 0) it is ignored. The product of the remaining feature likelihood ratios give the likelihood ratio of that trace. The average of the N trace likelihood ratios gives the odds that the test word was on the list. If the odds is greater than 1.0 an old response is made, otherwise a new response is made. The equations giving the odds are based on an ideal Bayesian decision derivation (for details, see Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). bREM is an extremely simple model with just three parameters, and was not meant to be a reasonably complete model of recognition.
 
D] What bREM predicts
 Given its simplicity and the many processes known to contribute to recognition that are missing from bREM it is remarkable that this model correctly predicts the main findings from recognition studies. In 1997 bREM was applied to studies using words as stimuli and quite accurately predicted effects of list length, list strength, study time, word frequency, and the amount of extra estimated variance of targets vs foils (termed zROC).  The 1997 and 1998 articles also discussed various extensions of bREM, without producing more than a few predictions. However, augmented versions of bREM were applied successfully to a number of other experimental recognition and recall paradigms. Examples include applications to studies of output interference (e.g. Criss, Malmbeg & Shiffrin, 2011), applications to modeling of response time and accuracy together, of signal-to-respond paradigms, and of recall and cued recall (Cox & Shiffrin, 2017), and applications to the evolution of event memory and knowledge together, to judgments of learned stimuli vs novel stimuli (known as lexical decision when the stimuli are words vs. nonwords) and to perceptual decisions (Nelson and Shiffrin (2013). 
Looking ahead to the  present modeling, it should be noted that predicting ‘list strength’ with bREM required strengthening a trace, and strengthening a trace required recalling it, because the value used for decision, the odds, is an average across all traces. Thus bREM had a role for recall in recognition, although all decisions were based on odds, interpreted as familiarity. .
In addition, bREM in its original form has been used successfully by Mohamed et al (submitted). Those researchers greatly extended the range of conditions to which bREM was applied: 12 item and 24 item lists of word and pictures and mixed lists of words and pictures length were studied. One group (a control) carried out single item old/new recognition as was predicted by bREM in 1997. New paradigms were used for the remaining groups: Two items were always presented for test, both old, one old and one new, or both new. In 2AFC the participant was asked to choose the item more likely to be old, even when both might be old or might be new. In 4WC the participant classified the two items as both old, left old and right new, left new and right old, or both new. Another group repeated all these conditions but judged items to be new rather than old (which prior research showed produced a different pattern of responses). Over the three experiments there were 288 different conditions with distinct results. bREM was used to predict every condition using the u and g parameter values from 1997, and using one c parameter value for words and another for pictures (because the standard sets of pictures were known to produce better performance than that for the standard set of words that were used). Applying bREM to tests of two items was straightforward: An odds is calculated for each item; in 2AFC the item with larger odds is chosen; in 4WC, each item’s odds is used to classify the pair, using the usual assumption that an old response is given for odds greater than 1.0. The patterns of correct and error responses for the 288 conditions were correctly predicted perfectly and the predictions were in most cases quite close quantitatively. Mohamed et al (submitted) concluded that such excellent predictions for such a large range of conditions by such a simple model (without tailoring parameter values to each condition) was unlikely to be an accident and suggested that the core processes in bREM, particularly the use of ratios for decision making (see Glanzer & Adams, 1985, Glanzer, Hilford & Maloney, 2009) were likely fundamental to the way humans recognize. It was these results and conclusions that led us to use bREM as a basis for further model development. The two studies to be described shortly were designed to provide data extending paradigms previously investigated, to point the way to extend recognition modeling generally, and specifically to extend recognition modeling by augmenting bREM. 
E] Augmented REM
When modeling the data from the two new studies, we do not aim to include all important processes missing from many recognition models, and missing specifically from bREM. That would be too large a step and lead to a model far too complex to evaluate or understand. However there are several processes known to be important for recognizing will be included in the new modeling, processes informed by the new studies. These are described next. 
1. Processes Using Context and Content Features. bREM has only one type of feature. Every model of memory finds it necessary to differentiate different types of features, at a minimum distinguishing context and content features.
Content Features. Term these T features. These features differentiate one picture from another. Thus for visually presented words such features might include the shape of the letters, the spelling of the word, and the semantic content (i.e. meaning). For pictures, we can again distinguish features, ranging from physical features like color and shape to high level meaning features (e.g. “a beach scene”; “a basketball game”). Cox and Shiffrin (2017) distinguished content feature types in a REM based model that focused mainly on the different ‘arrival’ times of the different feature types. Much research over the years not aimed at dynamics and not based on REM has taken feature differences into account. We cite only one recent publication by Guitard, Saint-Aubin, Reid, and Jamiesn (2025), although there are too many to count. The present research and modeling does not manipulate content feature types and does not consider the dynamics of retrieval of different feature types, because the stimuli are pictures randomly selected from a data base of pictures of a similar type. Thus the present extensions of bREM treat all content features as one type, denoted T.
Context Features. Context features are needed to focus retrieval on a specific set of memory traces. Context features are assumed capable of identifying the situation at the time of storage, but in most studies are not explicitly identified or manipulated. Such features might represent the physical environment and the internal state of a participant.
In many studies including the present ones that specific set is the pictures studied on the most recent list presented. That context features are critical for storing traces and retrieving them from memory has been a staple of memory theorizing forever (e.g. see James, 1890, chapter XVI; Bower, 1972; Tulving, 1974). There are an almost uncountable number of event traces stored in a human’s lifetime, and some means is needed to focus retrieval on just a small subset of these. A general discussion of context and its role in discriminating sets of pictures separated temporally is found in Klein, Shiffrin and Criss (2007); this chapter cites numerous examples of articles aimed at context and its roles, and presents some new studies as well.
The distinction between content and context is not precise, primarily because items can be associated together (see the discussion of associations later in this report). E.g. the ‘Temporal Context Model’ (TCM; Howard & Kahana, 2002) defines local context for a list item in terms of the items studied earlier on that list. Alternatively, if one elaborated a model to include associations between items as features, and/or assume associations between traces, then one might consider the role of items studied earlier as content (or as both as in Murnane, Phelps & Malmberg, 1999).
2. Use of Content and Context Features to Retrieve. When retrieval takes place one would expect that both context and content features would be used together to probe memory (as in Cox & Shiffrin, 2017). However, Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997) proposed a simpler-to-implement approach in which context features alone are used in a first stage of retrieval to determine which memory traces are activated and passed to a second stage. The traces activated are those with context features sufficiently similar to the probe context features. Each trace activated is passed to a second stage. The second stage uses content features only, as in bREM. In this approach context is used as a ‘filter’ in stage one to activate traces and pass them to the second stage only if their context is similar enough to the probe context. We have observed that the one stage and two stage approaches usually give similar results (at least for predicting choice probabilities). Thus the present research uses the two stage approach. (The two stage model should not be considered two successive stages each taking time, but rather a convenient approximation to a simultaneous use of context and content.)
3. Processes Identifying Changes of Context. Context can be considered to change within list as the pictures are successively studied (as in TCM), to change between list study and test of that list, to change during a period of testing, to change  between one list test and study of the next, to change after all the initial set of lists is completed and before a final test of all seen pictures is given (as in the present studies), and to change during final testing. Many of these changes of context are needed to deal with the data from the present studies.
Changes of context are generally considered critical to explain a good deal of long-term forgetting. Events are stored with current context, context that usually changes until testing occurs. There is a tendency to retrieve using the changed context available at test, resulting in forgetting (see Estes, 1950; Estes & Burke, 1953). A number of studies have shown that use of initially stored context to retrieve produces better memory than the use of changed context to retrieve (e.g. Smith & Vela, 2001). The improvements are generally modest in size, likely because the studies manipulate just one context factor when both storage and retrieval use multiple context factors. Murnane, Phelps and Malmberg (1999; the ICE model) show that the benefits of context reconstruction in recognition testing are larger when context and content are stored in a trace in integrated rather than incidental fashion. Bauml, Meixensperger and Hirsch (in press, and prior papers) have shown that much forgetting in cued recall after significant delays can be overcome by testing that allows reconstruction of stored context. Their studies show that such reconstruction does not happen when delay until test is less than an hour. The present studies use testing in a single session shorter than an hour, so the delays are not long enough for us to add context reconstruction to the model.
4. Storage of traces during testing. Testing a picture is an event and like all events gets stored. Thus when a foil is tested and judged new, a test trace could be stored in a manner similar to storage of a studied picture. When a test picture has an existing trace in a context similar to the test context, and the existing trace is recalled, it is not clear how much information about the test is added to the existing trace (see the next section discussing ‘strengthening’) and how much information is stored in the new trace (see Hintzman, Curran & Oppy, 1992; Malmberg, Holden & Shiffrin, 1994). To explain list-strength effects Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997, 1998) assumed that new test traces would not be stored in most cases when an existing strength was strengthened. It is not clear that such an assumption is necessary: Strengthening an existing trace might lead to less attention and a lesser amount of storage in a new trace. It likely would be hard to distinguish a failure to store a new trace from storage of a weak trace. It is convenient in the present modeling to store every study and test event in the same manner. In addition, recall of an existing trace may occur during study or test, and cause the recalled trace to be strengthened, as described in the next section. 
Storage of a new test trace plays a role in producing output interference (OI). OI Storing test traces increases the total number of activated traces, increasing effective list length, and thereby reducing performance. OI has been demonstrated in recognition testing (Criss, Malmberg & Shiffrin, 2011; Malmberg, Criss, Gangwani, Shiffrin, 2012; Malmberg, Lehman, Annis, Criss & Shiffrin, 2014). These authors  modeled the findings of OI by including in the model storage of test traces ( and strengthening of existing traces, as described in the next section). However, list length effects tend to be small in magnitude (see Osth & Dennis, 2015), and there are other factors that might play a larger role in producing OI. Osth, Jansson, Dennis and Heathcote (2018) tested several models and concluded context drift was the most important factor producing OI, but these authors failed to consider the role of strengthening traces (see next).
5. Processes that Strengthen Existing Traces. Raiijmakers and Shiffrin (1980, 1981) laid out the role of strengthening existing traces in recall tasks (referred to as incrementing). The sampling was assumed proportional to the strengths of the traces. Proportional sampling might or might not be the best choice but was chosen for simplicity and convenience. What is more important is that the choice be probabilistic. Sampling the strongest trace might work reasonably well in practice, but is hard to justify (for further discussion see Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997). 
Strengthening of existing traces was the basis of the explanation formed by Shiffrin, Ratcliff and Clark (1990) to explain the list-strength effect (LSE). LSE referred to findings that storing some items more strongly at study helped rather than harmed recognition of the remaining items (Ratcliff, Clark & Shiffrin, 1990). The authors argued that strengthening traces made them less similar to other traces, causing ‘differentiation’ and reducing confusion. Indeed differentiation due to differential study time is embedded in bREM.


It is important to implement strengthening of traces that occurs during testing. The concept is similar: In parallel with computation of familiarity (odds in bREM) a trace might be recalled. The recalled trace will be augmented by addition of features of the test picture so that the trace and test picture become more similar. If the trace in question was produced by study of a target being tested, the added features will improve the accuracy of a later test of that picture. The strengthening also makes that trace less similar to others (differentiation). By itself this factor will improve recognition of other pictures yet to be tested, but that is not the only result of strengthening as discussed next.


 In most studies there are a good number of false alarms when foils are tested. In such cases (other than the present Experiment 2) any recalled trace will be an existing one that was produced by some previously seen picture other than the current foil. Strengthening its trace will add features of the test foil thereby degrading the similarity of the trace to the picture that produced it, and lowering performance when and if that picture is later tested. This factor combined with the increased number of traces stored during testing can dominate the gains from differentiation, and produce OI. How much of the OI is due to these two different factors is a quantitative question to be answered by modeling.
6. The use of recall in making recognition decisions. The use of recall in recognition studies has been studied and modeled for many years (Mandler, Pearlstone & Koopmans, 1969; Juola, Fischler, Wood & Atkinson, 1971; see the extended discussion in Gillund & Shiffrin, 1984). In recent years, a design used to address this issue required participants to judge whether they ‘remember’ (i.e. remember details and context of storage) or ‘know’ (i.e. have a sense of familiarity) an item judged old (e.g., Gardiner & Java, 1990). Gillund and Shiffrin (1984) discussed the need for a process of recall-to-reject when foils are highly similar to targets, and cited relevant research. Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997) embedded differentiation in bREM, based on earlier research by Ratcliff, Clark, and Shiffrin (1990); differentiation required strengthening traces recalled during list study; they also discussed the need for recall operating during testing
This extremely truncated review of results and modeling suggests the roles of several important recognition processes missing from bREM that will be included in the modeling of the present studies. This listing is in no way meant to cover all the important processes that ought to be incorporated in a reasonably complete account of recognition. As an illustration of this point we next mention briefly four processes not incorporated in the extended REM model for the present studies. These are mentioned just to make the point that the present modeling is meant to take a modest step toward a complete recognition model. A] Associations between items studied. These play an important role in the modeling of TCM (Howard & Kahana, 2002, and its successor models) where they act as context. Alternatively associations can be treated as content, but this opens up many avenues for modeling and we cannot cover them in this report (see Cox & Shiffrin, 2017; Cox & Criss, 2020; Cox, 2024).B] Distinctions among content features. Low level features like shape and color play quite a different role than high level features like semantics and meaning, This is again a large field that would open up many avenues for modeling. C] Dynamics. Different feature types likely arrive in a form that can be used at different points in time (e.g. shape features can be used prior to meaning features). Dynamics played a key role in the modeling of Cox and Shiffrin (2017).D] Reconstruction of context after significant delay until test. E.g. see Bauml, Meixensperger and Hirsch (in press).
We turn now to the designs of the studies, and the rationale for those designs.
Overview of the Designs of the Two Experiments
Each study began with ten study-test recognition lists. Each study list contained new pictures not seen earlier in the experiment. Each list was followed by an arithmetic task to clear short-term memory – the present studies and modeling is aimed to explain recognition of events in long-term memory. Following the arithmetic are a series of old/new recognition tests, half from the list just studied (targets) and half not from the list just studied (foils). This procedure matches that from most recognition studies.
However, we are presently interested in context change and event memory that extends back in time past the current study list. Thus in the present designs there is a final testing phase after the ten initial lists have been studied and tested. In this final phase, 50% of the test pictures are targets. Targets are defined as any picture seen in some form in the initial ten lists: Such targets included pictures studied but not tested, pictures studied and tested, and pictures tested only as foils. The final testing phase also included 50% new foils, pictures not seen previously in any form, so that as usual, half the tests are targets and half foils. Presuming that context changes from list to list, and between initial lists and final testing, this design allows us to assess such changes.
In addition, we wanted to assess the possibility that participants can make use of the storage context during final testing. Thus in Experiment 1, three different orders of final testing were used: random, forward and backward: in forward pictures seen on list 1 were targets for the initial set of final tests, then pictures seen on list 2, etc., and participants were informed thus was the case for each set of such tests; in backward pictures seen on list 10 were targets for the initial set of final tests, then pictures seen on list 9, etc. and again participants were informed of the list of origin of the targets before each set of final tests. In random participants learned after each response what was the list of origin of each target. This manipulation was not used in Experiment 2, because the number of other conditions varied in that study were so large it would have been unfeasible to vary also order of final testing.
We are also interested in the joint use of familiarity and recall in recognition studies. In the usual paradigms these produce highly correlated responding, because a highly familiar picture is one likely to be recalled, and vice versa. That was the case for the present Experiment 1. The high correlation makes it difficult to assess the processes of familiarity and recall separately. Thus in Experiment 2 three fifths of the foils following each of the initial lists are pictures not seen on the current study list, but instead seen in some form on the prior list. Such foils are highly familiar, and discriminating them from current list targets that are also highly familiar likely requires a decision based on more than familiarity. We propose that recall processes are used: In parallel with the computation of familiarity a memory trace is often recalled. The contents of that recalled trace can be evaluated and sometimes the contents contain information about the list of origin of that trace. When that information indicates that the list of origin was the prior list, and not the current list, then a picture with high familiarity can be rejected as a target. In the design of Experiment 2 one source of list-of-origin information is information that the picture producing that trace had been tested, because a current list target had not yet been tested. Whatever the source of list-of-origin information, this process of rejecting a highly familiar test picture is known as “recall to reject” (e.g. Rotello, Macmillan & Tassel, 2000). 
Methods
Statistical Analysis
Model Specification 
Recognition accuracy was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial error distribution and logit link function, implemented in R (Version 4.3.1; R Core Team, 2024) using the lme4 package (Version 1.1-35.1; Bates et al., 2015). All models were fitted using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno quasi-Newton optimization algorithm (BOBYQA; Powell, 2009). Statistical significance was evaluated at α = .05. Item-type-specific trends were estimated using the emmeans package (Version 1.8.9; Lenth, 2023), and confidence intervals were computed using the Wald method.
Fixed and Random Effects
Position effects were modeled using orthogonal polynomial contrasts (linear and quadratic terms) to capture both monotonic trends and curvilinear patterns across serial positions. Fixed effects included position terms (study position, test position, or list number, depending on the analysis) fully crossed with item type (e.g., target vs. foil for initial tests; ST vs. SO vs. TO vs. FTO for final tests). For the final test order analysis in Experiment 1, an additional ordering-type factor distinguished effects by final test position from effects by initial list position.
All models included random intercepts for participants to account for individual differences in overall performance. Random effects structures were selected following the principle of maximal random effects justified by design (Barr et al., 2013), with adjustments made when models failed to converge. Specifically, random slopes for position effects were included when (a) position varied within participants, (b) individual differences in position sensitivity were theoretically plausible, and (c) the model converged successfully [FOOTNOTE: Random slopes for position effects were included when theoretically justified (e.g., individual differences in sensitivity to serial position) and when models converged without boundary issues. Random slope specifications varied across analyses based on model convergence and data structure.]. When maximal models did not converge despite extended iterations and alternative optimizers, we simplified by first removing random slope correlations, then removing random slopes for higher-order polynomial terms (quadratic before linear), and finally removing random slopes entirely if necessary. For Experiment 1's final test combined order analysis, the complex factorial design (three conditions × two ordering types × four item types) required a simplified random effects structure (random intercepts only) to achieve convergence given the large number of fixed effect parameters. All fixed effects were retained as specified by the experimental design and theoretical framework.
Experiment-Specific Design Considerations 
Experiment 2 Item Type Categories. For Experiment 2 analyses involving confusing foils, item type categories were expanded to include STn (studied-tested on list n), SOn (studied-only on list n), TOn (tested-only on list n), in addition to the standard ST, SO, TO, and FTO categories. These expanded categories account for items that appeared across consecutive lists during the initial phase.
Final Test Position Binning. Final test positions were binned into 10 temporal bins (1–8 with 49 items each, 9–10 with 50 items each) for Experiment 2 to create sufficient observations per bin while maintaining temporal resolution.
Reference Category Selection. Because FTO items have no list number variation, STn served as the reference category for slope comparisons in interaction terms, while FTO remained the intercept reference in Experiment 2 analyses. This dual-reference approach allows for meaningful interpretation of both intercept differences (using FTO as baseline) and slope differences (using STn as baseline for list number effects).
Convergence and Model Diagnostics 
Convergence was assessed using two criteria: (1) relative gradient magnitude (<0.001 recommended threshold) and (2) positive-definiteness of the Hessian matrix. Most models converged successfully with relative gradients well below the 0.001 threshold. However, several complex models showed convergence warnings with relative gradients between 0.002 and 0.006, particularly for Experiment 2 analyses and Experiment 1's combined final test order analysis (relative gradient = 0.022) [FOOTNOTE: The Experiment 1 combined final test order analysis examined a complex 3-way interaction (condition × item type × ordering type) with four item types and three conditions, requiring estimation of numerous parameters with limited observations per cell. Despite the convergence warning (relative gradient = 0.022), parameter estimates were stable and interpretable, and simplified models produced substantively identical conclusions. For Experiment 2, models with confusing foils (items appearing across consecutive lists) showed elevated relative gradients (0.002–0.006), likely due to the sparse and unbalanced distribution of these item types across positions.]. For models that did not fully converge, we (a) verified that parameter estimates were stable across optimization methods (including Nelder-Mead and BOBYQA with extended iterations up to 200,000 function evaluations), (b) confirmed that fixed effect estimates and standard errors were consistent with simplified models, and (c) used fallback variance estimation when the Hessian was not positive definite (Bates et al., 2015). All reported results represent the best-fitting models achievable given the complex factorial designs and data structure, and we interpret results cautiously when convergence was suboptimal.
Computational Model Implementation
All computational model simulations were performed through simulation-based estimation implemented in the Julia programming language (Version 1.10.9; Bezanson et al., 2017). The parameter values were not fully optimized, but accepted and used to make predictions when the fit to the observed data was judged qualitatively acceptable. Model predictions are shown alongside empirical data in all figures to facilitate comparison.
Method of Experiment 1
Participants. 
Participants were recruited through Prolific Academic (prolific.com), an online research platform. Eligibility requirements included: (1) residence in the United States, (2) minimum age of 18 years, (3) English fluency, and (4) a minimum 98% approval rating on previous Prolific studies to ensure data quality. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Indiana University (Protocol #18431). Participants provided informed consent before beginning the experiment. Compensation was provided at an hourly rate of $10.50, with an estimated completion time of approximately 35 minutes (actual payment of approximately $6.13).
Stimuli and Apparatus
Stimuli. The experiment used 2,361 unique color photographs of everyday objects and scenes selected from a stimulus set of 2,400 pictures collected by Brady et al. (2008) from Talia Konkle's laboratory. The pictures for each participant were randomly selected from these. Then the pictures for each condition were randomly selected from this subset.
Software and Technical Environment. The experiment was implemented using jsPsych version 7.3.3 (de Leeuw, 2015), a JavaScript library for web-based behavioral experiments providing millisecond-level timing precision. The experiment was hosted on the Cognition.run platform. Technical requirements included desktop computer access and Google Chrome browser usage, which were verified programmatically. Participants were required to use full-screen mode throughout the experiment to minimize distractions. All stimulus images were preloaded before testing to ensure smooth presentation timing.
Design and Procedure. The experiment employed a within-subjects design with two main phases: (1) ten initial lists with study-test cycles and (2) a final recognition test with testing order varied between groups.
Phase 1: Initial Study-Test Cycles
Participants received detailed instructions before beginning the experimental procedure. These instructions explained that the experiment would consist of a practice trial followed by ten formal study-test cycles. For each cycle, participants were told they would complete three sequential stages: first, studying a list of pictures that would appear one at a time on the screen; second, viewing a series of numbers to mentally sum and then typing their answer; and third, completing a recognition test for pictures, pressing the "J" key to indicate they had seen a picture in the just-studied list and pressing the "F" key to indicate they had not seen it in that list. Participants were also told that accumulated accuracy would be displayed on the screen trial by trial as they progressed through the study. The instructions emphasized that uncertainty about many test pictures was normal and encouraged participants to make their best guess in such cases.
Practice Trial. After providing informed consent and passing browser compatibility checks, participants completed a single practice trial to familiarize themselves with the experimental procedure. This practice trial followed the identical three-stage structure described below but served only as training. Upon completion of the practice trial, participants received brief instructions stating that they had finished the practice and could begin the formal study when ready by pressing the enter key.
Following the practice trial, participants completed ten formal study-test cycles. Each cycle followed an identical three-stage structure.
Study Phase. After a 1,000 ms fixation cross, 20 unique pictures were presented sequentially. Each picture appeared for 2,000 ms with 100 ms inter-stimulus intervals consisting of blank screens. Participants were instructed to study each picture as it appeared.
Distractor Phase. The distractor phase served to clear short-term memory and create a retention interval between study and test. Following study, participants saw a 3,000 ms screen displaying the instructional prompt "Summing up the digits as they appear," followed by a 1,000 ms fixation cross. Then eight randomly selected digits (ranging from 4 to 9) were presented sequentially, with each digit displayed for 2,000 ms and 1,000 ms gaps between consecutive digits. After all eight digits had been presented, participants saw the prompt "TYPE THE SUM" and entered their calculated sum using the number keys. If participants entered non-numeric characters, a warning message appeared and they were required to re-enter their response. Once a valid numeric response was submitted, participants received immediate feedback displaying either "CORRECT!" or "INCORRECT!" for 2,000 ms.
Recognition Test Phase. Following the distractor task, participants saw a 3,000 ms instructional prompt stating "Now judge if you have seen this picture from the list you JUST studied before the digits," followed by a 1,000 ms fixation cross. Then 20 pictures were presented sequentially for old/new recognition judgments. Of these 20 test pictures, 10 were targets randomly selected from the 20 pictures in the preceding study list, and 10 were foils (novel images that had not been studied). The targets and foils were randomly intermixed. For each picture, participants pressed "J" to indicate "old" (previously studied in the just-completed list) or "F" to indicate "new" (not studied in that list). Participants had up to 3,500 ms to make each response. Following each response, accumulated accuracy for the current recognition test was displayed on the screen (e.g., "Your accumulated accuracy for this trial is: 75%"). Responses faster than 150 ms triggered a "Too fast!" warning message displayed for 1,500 ms. Failures to respond within the 3,500 ms time limit prompted a "You need to respond faster!" message displayed for 1,500 ms.
Picture Types Created by the Procedure. This procedure created four distinct picture types that would be relevant for the subsequent final recognition test. Studied-and-Tested (ST) pictures were pictures that appeared in the study phase and were subsequently tested as targets in the recognition test. Studied-Only (SO) pictures were pictures that appeared in the study phase but were not selected as targets for the recognition test. Test-Only (TO) pictures were pictures that appeared only as foils during the initial recognition testing phase and were never studied. Final-Test-Only (FTO) pictures were pictures that appeared exclusively as foils in the final recognition test and had never appeared during any of the ten initial study-test cycles.
Phase 2: Final Recognition Test
Following completion of the tenth study-test cycle, participants transitioned to a final recognition test with modified task instructions. Participants were presented with detailed instructions explaining that they would see a very long series of test pictures. They were explicitly instructed to press the "J" key (old) for any image they had seen at any point during the experiment, whether it had been studied or tested on any list. The instructions emphasized, with highlighted red text, that old responses would be required for all pictures seen earlier, specifically  including pictures that were not studied but were tested for the first time during the initial recognition tests. Participants were instructed to press the "F" key (new) only for completely novel images that had never appeared before in the experiment.
Participants were further informed that the feedback structure would differ substantially from the initial test phase. Rather than receiving running accuracy feedback after each response, they would instead be shown information about each picture's experimental history. For old pictures (pictures that had actually appeared in the experiment), they would be told which list number the picture had appeared on, with list numbers ranging from 1 for the first list to 10 for the last list. For novel foils (pictures that had never appeared), they would be informed that the picture had never been seen previously. In addition, in the forward and backward conditions participants were informed prior to each block of tests which of the ten lists the targets would be from (see a more detailed description below).
Test Composition
The final test comprised 420 trials: 210 old pictures drawn from previous phases and 210 completely novel foils. From each of the 10 study lists, 7 pictures were randomly sampled from each of the three picture types that had actually appeared during the initial phase (ST, SO, and TO), yielding 210 old pictures total (10 lists × 3 picture types × 7 pictures per type = 210). These 210 old pictures were randomly intermixed with 210 novel FTO foils that had never appeared in any prior phase of the experiment.
Between-Subjects Presentation Order Conditions. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions that determined the presentation sequence of the 420 final test trials. The conditions differed in how old pictures from different initial lists were temporally organized within the final test.
Forward Condition. Participants in the forward condition were informed at the outset that the final test would progress chronologically through the initial lists, beginning with pictures from List 1 and proceeding sequentially through List 10. The 420 trials were organized into ten blocks of 42 trials each, with each block corresponding to one of the initial study lists in forward temporal order. Before each block of 42 trials, participants received an explicit notification indicating which list number the old pictures in the upcoming block would be drawn from (e.g., "The pictures you studied or tested in the upcoming block are from List 3"). Each block of 42 trials contained 21 old pictures from the designated list (7 ST pictures, 7 SO pictures, and 7 TO pictures) randomly intermixed with 21 novel FTO foils. Within each block of 42 trials, all pictures were presented in random order.
Backward Condition. Participants in the backward condition were informed that the final test would progress in reverse chronological order, beginning with pictures from List 10 and proceeding backward sequentially through List 1. The 420 trials were organized into ten blocks of 42 trials each, with each block corresponding to one of the initial study lists in reverse temporal order. Before each block of 42 trials, participants received an explicit notification indicating which list number the old pictures in the upcoming block would be drawn from (e.g., "The pictures you studied or tested in the upcoming block are from List 8"). Each block of 42 trials contained 21 old pictures from the designated list (7 ST pictures, 7 SO pictures, and 7 TO pictures) randomly intermixed with 21 novel FTO foils. Within each block of 42 trials, all pictures were presented in random order.
Random Condition. Participants in the random condition received all 420 trials in completely random order with no organizational structure by list. These participants were not given any advance information about which list old pictures were drawn from before any test trial. However, they received the same post-response feedback as participants in the other conditions, with feedback indicating the list number for old pictures or noting that novel foils had never been seen previously.
Final Test Trial Procedure
Each trial in the final recognition test followed a standard format. Images were presented individually with a response window of up to 4,000 ms. Participants pressed "J" (old) or "F" (new) using the same response mapping as the initial test phase. Responses faster than 150 ms triggered a "Too fast!" warning displayed for 1,500 ms, while failures to respond within the 4,000 ms time limit prompted a "You need to respond faster!" message displayed for 1,500 ms.
Following each response, the test image reappeared for 500 ms with text displayed below indicating its experimental history. For old pictures, the feedback specified the list on which the picture had appeared (e.g., "This picture had been studied or tested on list 7"). For novel foils, the feedback stated "This picture had never been seen previously." This informative feedback served dual purposes: maintaining participant engagement during the lengthy 420-trial final test and enhancing attention to the temporal structure of the original list presentation order. After the 500 ms feedback display, a 100 ms blank inter-trial interval preceded the next trial.
Dependent measures. 
Accuracy and response times were recorded for all responses, but only accuracy is reported and used for modeling. The median response times for every condition are reported in Appendix XXX for archival purposes. For each of the ten initial lists, recognition accuracy was calculated as the proportion of hits (correct old judgments for target tests) and proportion of correct rejections (correct new responses to foil test). For targets, hits were analyzed by the serial position within the study list. Performance on final tests was also reported by hits and correct rejections, broken down by order of final tests and for targets by order of initial list and serial positions within list. The figures showing the hits and correct rejections, and the tables of hits and correct rejections in Appendix YYY, give for each condition the standard error of the mean, across participants. 
Method of Experiment 2
Participants
Participant recruitment and eligibility criteria were identical to Experiment 1, with one additional exclusion criterion: participants who had completed Experiment 1 were excluded to prevent contamination from prior exposure to the stimulus set. All procedures were approved by the same Institutional Review Board protocol (Indiana University Protocol #18431), and informed consent procedures matched those of Experiment 1. Compensation was provided at the same hourly rate as Experiment 1 ($10.50), with an estimated completion time of approximately 45 minutes (actual payment of approximately $7.88).
Stimuli and Apparatus
Stimuli. Stimuli were drawn from the same database as Experiment 1: unique color photographs from the Brady et al. (2008) stimulus set. However, Experiment 2 required more unique images due to the systematic manipulation of confusing foils. For Experiment 2, exactly 369 unique pictures were required for the initial study-test phase: 45 pictures for List 1 and 324 pictures for Lists 2-10 (36 new pictures per list). List 1 required more new pictures than subsequent lists because it lacked confusing foils carried forward from previous lists. An additional 246 completely novel images served as final test foils, yielding 615 total unique images.
Software and Technical Environment. The experimental implementation used the same jsPsych version 7.3.3 framework as Experiment 1, with identical technical requirements (desktop computer access, Google Chrome browser, full-screen mode). All stimulus images were preloaded before testing to ensure smooth presentation timing, following the same protocol as Experiment 1. All experimental timing parameters were identical to Experiment 1. Study images were presented for 2,000 ms with 100 ms inter-stimulus intervals, preceded by 1,000 ms fixation crosses. Distractor phases used the same timing (digits presented for 2,000 ms each with 1,000 ms gaps, followed by 2,000 ms feedback displays). Recognition test response windows matched Experiment 1 (3,500 ms for initial tests, 4,000 ms for final test), with identical quality control measures (150 ms response time warnings displayed for 1,500 ms, and non-response prompts). Final test trials included 500 ms feedback following each response, as in Experiment 1.
Design and Procedure.
The experiment employed the same within-subjects design structure as Experiment 1: 10 initial study-test cycles followed by a final recognition test. The critical difference was the systematic manipulation of foil composition during initial test phases. Starting with List 2, three-fifths of the foils on each test list had been seen on the prior list, creating confusing foils that participants were instructed to reject as "new" despite having encountered them previously.
Experimental Design Structure. Stimuli were organized into units of exactly three pictures each. This organization addressed two essential requirements: ensuring adequate statistical power (minimum 3 pictures per condition per list, yielding 30 data points per condition across all lists per participant) and facilitating counterbalancing of specific images across experimental roles between participants.
The base picture types for any given list n were defined as follows: SO (Studied-Only) pictures were studied but not tested as targets in the initial lists. TO (Test-Only) pictures appeared only as foils during testing in the initial lists. ST (Studied-and-Tested) pictures were both studied and tested as targets in the initial lists. FTO (Final-Test-Only) pictures appeared only as foils in the final test and were never studied in the initial lists.
In addition to these base categories, temporal markers were used to track picture carryover between lists. Pictures appearing only in their current list were denoted as SO, TO, or ST. Pictures from list n that were reassigned to serve as confusing foils in list n+1were denoted SO(n), TO(n), or ST(n). Conversely, confusing foils in the current list that originated from the preceding list (n-1) were denoted SO(n-1), TO(n-1), or ST(n-1). The key constraint was that pictures could only be carried forward once to serve as confusing foils in the immediately subsequent list, preventing cumulative exposure effects across multiple lists.

Participants received detailed initial instructions explaining the three-stage structure of each cycle. The instructions stated that participants would see a list of 30 pictures appearing one at a time on the screen, followed by a series of numbers to add mentally before typing the sum, and finally a series of test pictures requiring old/new recognition judgments. Participants were told to press the "J" key if they recognized a picture as one they had seen in the current list and to press the "F" key if they did not recognize it as being from the current list. The instructions emphasized that accumulated accuracy would be displayed throughout the experiment and that participants should try to achieve average performance higher than 60%, with chance level being 50%. The instructions also noted that participants would begin with a practice list followed by nine formal test lists, and importantly, that uncertainty about some test pictures was normal but they should make their best guess.
Practice Trial and Transition to Formal Lists. After providing informed consent and passing browser compatibility checks, participants completed List 1 as a practice trial. This initial list served to familiarize participants with the experimental procedure while also introducing the first set of pictures that would later serve as confusing foils in List 2. 
Prior to List 2, participants received instructions informing them that some test pictures in subsequent lists might include images they had encountered in previous lists, and that they should respond 'new' to such pictures since they had not been studied in the current list.
Study-Test Cycle Structure. The ten study-test cycles followed the identical three-stage structure as Experiment 1: study phase, distractor task, and recognition phase.
Study Phase. Procedures were identical to Experiment 1, except that 30 pictures (rather than 20) were presented sequentially. Each picture appeared for 2,000 ms with 100 ms inter-stimulus intervals, preceded by a 1,000 ms fixation cross. For List 1, all 30 studied pictures were new images that had never appeared previously in the experiment. For Lists 2-10, all 30 studied pictures were also new images that had never been studied before, though some of these pictures might later appear as confusing foils in the subsequent list.
Distractor Phase. The arithmetic task was identical to Experiment 1, involving the presentation and summing of eight randomly selected digits to prevent rehearsal between study and test phases. The timing and feedback procedures matched those of Experiment 1 exactly.
Recognition Phase. Following the distractor task, participants saw a 3,000 ms instructional prompt stating "Now judge if you have seen this picture from the list you JUST studied before the digits," followed by a 1,000 ms fixation cross. Then 30 pictures were presented sequentially for old/new recognition judgments. Recognition test feedback was identical to Experiment 1, with running accuracy displayed and updated immediately after each response within the current list (e.g., "Your accumulated accuracy for this list is: 75%"). Response timing, warnings, and quality control measures matched Experiment 1.
For List 1, the 30 test pictures consisted of targets randomly selected from the 30 studied pictures and novel foils, using the same response mapping as Experiment 1 ("J" for old, "F" for new). For Lists 2-10, each test contained targets from studied pictures, novel foils, and critically, confusing foils representing different exposure histories from the previous list. Specifically, each test in Lists 2-10 included confusing foils of type SO(n-1), TO(n-1), and ST(n-1), where n-1 refers to the immediately preceding list. Participants were expected to correctly reject these confusing foils as "new" despite having encountered them on the previous list, because they had not been studied in the current list.
Phase 2: Final Recognition Test
Final test procedures closely paralleled Experiment 1, with modifications to accommodate the confusing foil manipulation. Participants received instructions identical in structure to Experiment 1: they were told they had completed the first part of the study and would now see a very long series of test pictures. They were explicitly instructed to press the "J" key (old) for any image encountered at any point during the initial study-test phase, whether it had been studied or tested on any list, and to press the "F" key (new) only for completely novel images. The instructions emphasized that accumulated accuracy would be presented after each response, as it had been during the initial test phase.
Test Composition. The final test comprised 492 trials: 246 pictures drawn from previous phases and 246 completely novel foils. The 246 old pictures were sampled at a two-thirds ratio from the 369 pictures that had appeared during the initial test phase, following the same balanced structure as Experiment 1 to ensure adequate representation of all picture types and exposure histories.
Test Order. All participants in Experiment 2 received pictures in a single randomized presentation order, with all previously encountered and novel pictures thoroughly intermixed throughout the test sequence. Unlike Experiment 1, which included forward, backward, and random presentation conditions, Experiment 2 employed only the random condition. This decision was made due to the many new conditions created by the confusing foil manipulation and the need to obtain reliable data for each condition. Future research would be valuable to examine confusing foils as in Experiment 2 while also implementing the test order manipulations of Experiment 1.
Final Test Procedure. Each trial followed the same format as Experiment 1. Images were presented individually with a response window of up to 4,000 ms. Participants pressed "J" (old) or "F" (new) using the same response mapping as the initial test phase. Responses faster than 150 ms triggered warnings, and non-responses prompted speed warnings, with timing identical to Experiment 1.
Following each response, participants received feedback adapted from the random order condition of Experiment 1. For pictures that had appeared during the initial phase, the feedback indicated the picture's specific experimental history. Because confusing foils appeared on multiple lists, the feedback for such pictures specified both list appearances (e.g., "This picture had been studied or tested on list 3 and list 4"). For pictures that appeared on only a single list, the feedback specified that list (e.g., "This picture had been studied or tested on list 7"). For novel foils, the feedback stated "This picture had never been seen previously." This informative feedback maintained engagement during the lengthy 492-trial final test while providing participants with information about the temporal structure of picture presentations.
Dependent Measures. Recognition accuracy and response times were recorded for each study-test cycle, with particular focus on false alarm rates for different confusing foil types (SO(n-1), TO(n-1), ST(n-1)). Overall recognition accuracy in the final test was analyzed by picture type and original exposure history, extending Experiment 1's analyses to examine how different prior exposure types influenced long-term recognition performance. The dependent measures paralleled those of Experiment 1, with additional analyses focusing on the confusing foil manipulations and their effects on both initial and final test performance.
Results 
We report results from two experiments examining recognition memory across multiple study-test lists. For each experiment, we first present data quality screening procedures, followed by analyses of within-list effects during initial testing (study position and test position effects), between-list effects during initial testing, and final test performance as a function of original study and test positions and list order. Empirical data are presented with model predictions from an augmented REM model in all figures. Statistical results are reported in text, with complete model summaries provided in the appendices.
Results of Experiment 1
Data Exclusion and Quality Control
A total of 206 participants completed the experiment (backward: n = 50; forward: n = 53; random: n = 103). Data quality screening followed standard cognitive-psychology procedures (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) overall accuracy or initial test performance > 2.5 SD below the sample mean, (b) median response time > 3 SD from the mean, (c) > 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, or (d) > 30% performance decline from initial to final test. Trials faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were also excluded.
Ten participants (4.9%) were removed for low accuracy or disengagement (e.g., rushed responding or extreme performance decline). One additional participant was retained despite extreme initial performance (z = –5.17) because accuracy improved to normal levels on the final test, response times were within the typical range, and the pattern reflected genuine learning rather than inattention.
The final sample included 196 participants (backward: 48; forward: 48; random: 100) contributing 126,876 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and received compensation consistent with institutional review board guidelines.
The main results will be displayed in figures as well as given as values in the appendices. The data are shown along with standard errors of the mean (across participants). 
Analysis Overview
The following analyses examine recognition accuracy across different phases and position types:
Initial Test: Study Position Effects. Study position effects during initial testing were examined using a GLMM with accuracy as the dependent variable, study position (linear and quadratic orthogonal polynomial terms) and item type (target vs. foil) as fixed effects, and random intercepts and slopes for study position by participant. This analysis tested whether serial position effects during encoding influenced immediate recognition performance and whether these effects differed for target recognition versus foil rejection.
Initial Test: Test Position Effects. Test position effects during initial testing were examined to assess whether output interference accumulated during the test phase itself and whether such interference differed for target recognition versus foil rejection. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, test position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type (target vs. foil) as fixed effects, and random intercepts and slopes for test position by participant.
Initial Test: Between-List Effects. Between-list effects during initial testing were examined to assess whether proactive interference accumulated across successive study-test lists and whether interference patterns differed for target recognition versus foil rejection. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, list number (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant.
Final Test: Study Position Effects. For items with study history (ST and SO), final test recognition was examined as a function of original study position within each initial list. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, study position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant. This analysis tested whether within-list serial position effects established during encoding persisted into delayed recognition and whether retrieval practice during initial testing modified these patterns.
Final Test: Test Position Effects. For items with initial test history (ST and TO), final test recognition was examined as a function of original test position within each initial list. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, test position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant. This analysis tested whether within-list output interference effects observed during initial testing persisted into delayed recognition.
Final Test: Combined Order Effects (Primary Analysis). Recognition in the final test was examined using a combined list-order analysis that accounted for the relationship between initial and final test order across presentation conditions. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, list order (linear and quadratic terms), condition (forward, backward, random), ordering type (by final test order vs. by initial list order), and item type as fixed effects, with all theoretically relevant interactions, and random intercepts by participant. In the forward condition, initial order equals final order; in the backward condition, initial order is the mirror of final order (list 1 initial = list 10 final); in the random condition, initial and final orders are uncorrelated. An ordering-type factor was introduced to distinguish effects by final test order (present in all conditions) from effects by initial list order (present only in the random condition, analyzed as additional observations).
Organization of Results
The results are organized as follows: For each experiment, we first examine within-list effects during initial testing (study position and test position effects), followed by between-list effects during initial testing. We then examine final test performance as a function of original study and test positions from the initial lists, followed by final test performance as a function of list order (both final test order and initial list order).

Results of Experiment 2
Data Exclusion and Quality Control
A total of 199 participants completed the experiment through online recruitment via Prolific. Data quality screening was conducted using multiple exclusion criteria following standard practices in cognitive psychology research (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). At the participant level, individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) below-chance performance (accuracy < 50%), indicating random responding; (b) overall task accuracy more than 2.5 standard deviations below the sample mean; (c) median response time more than 3 standard deviations from the sample mean, indicating atypical response patterns; or (d) more than 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, indicating insufficient processing time or rushed responding. At the trial level, responses faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were excluded from all analyses, consistent with established response window parameters.
Thirteen participants (6.5%) were excluded based on participant-level criteria. Specifically, nine participants exhibited below-chance performance (M = 0.48, SD = 0.01, range = 0.48–0.50), indicating they were responding randomly or not engaging meaningfully with the task. Two additional participants exhibited extremely low accuracy (22.9% and 29.1%, both >3 SD below the mean) combined with below-chance performance. Three participants were excluded due to extreme response time outliers (median RTs of 281 ms, 1,654 ms, and 1,669 ms; |z| > 3.3), with one also exhibiting rushed responding (19.5% of responses < 200 ms). At the trial level, 479 trials (0.31% of all test trials) were excluded due to response times outside the 150–3,500 ms window (355 trials < 150 ms, 124 trials > 3,500 ms).
The final sample consisted of 186 participants contributing 156,214 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated for their time in accordance with institutional review board guidelines.

Initial Within-List Test Position Effects 
Figure 7.1.b shows test position effects conditioned on the various types of test pictures. The solid lines show current list target Hs and current list foil CRs. These show roughly the pattern of Experiment 1, with Hs rising and CRs falling as tests continue. The explanation for these results from both studies will be presented in the modeling presentation.
The most interesting results are in the dashed lines that show correct rejections (CRs) for current list foils that had been seen on the prior trial (termed ‘confusing foils’). Due to their presence on the prior trial these test pictures are highly familiar and as a result are given many false alarms (FAs), or equivalently are given low CRs. The most familiar pictures are those studied and tested on the prior trial (as demonstrated by final test results to be shown shortly). If familiarity (REM odds, or some other measure of degree of familiarity) were used to respond, these pictures would be given the greatest false alarms (FAs), and equivalently the lowest CRs. Yet these very familiar pictures produce CRs higher than pictures from the prior trial that were studied only, and not much lower than CRs for foils seen on the prior trial. Such results suggest that participants faced with highly familiar pictures that are not from the current list start using a recall process: They recall a trace likely to be one produced by the test picture and assess the trace contents. When they find information in that trace that identifies the origin of the picture producing the trace to be the prior list, they ignore the high familiarity and respond new. This is a process known as ‘recall to reject’ (e.g. Rotello, Macmillan & Tassel, 2000). This process is described in detail in the modeling section.

Modeling and Discussion Experiments 1 and 2
Overview
The starting point is the simple three parameter REM model (bREM) of Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997) that has proved remarkably capable of capturing the major trends of recognition studies (e.g. 288 conditions in the research of Mohamed et al., in press).  bREM is missing many processes known or very likely to play important roles in recognizing. Thus the present studies were aimed to provide data helping to augment bREM by incorporating important missing processes. bREM was described in the introduction and need not be repeated here.     
It is important to repeat that the goal of the modeling is qualitative, not a model that predicts the data as precisely as possible. There are enormous differences in performance among participants seen in Figures 6 and 11. The model predicts data that is a complex mixture averaged across not just across participants but across pictures, and across other variables that might affect performance. At best such a model is a crude approximation, so we aim to  predict the major observed trends rather than quantitative details.
The basic assumptions of bREM are used in the present modeling, with some elaborations described in the following sections. To be specific, each picture is represented by 23 content features (termed T), and two types of context features (delineated shortly): 28 CC features and 28 UC features. Each feature value for each picture is sampled independently from a geometric distribution with parameter value g = 0.3.  Each feature of a picture studied is stored (or not) with probability u*= 0.4. If stored the probability the value will be stored correctly is c = 0.85 (Exp. 1) and 0.70 (Exp. 2) 
Augmenting REM with processes missing from bREM
General background concerning bREM and some processes that bREM is missing has been covered in the introduction. Here we give more detail, and the way that the model implements these processes.
Context Features
A. Context features used to retrieve and activate traces.  As suggested by Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997) CC and UC context features are used alone, without content features, in a first stage of retrieval. This stage determines which traces are sufficiently similar in context to the context in the retrieval probe to become activated and then passed to the second stage. This two stage approach is meant as an easy-to-implement approximation to a single stage system in which context and content are used together to probe memory (as was used in the dynamic model of Cox & Shiffrin, 2017). The first stage uses the regular REM machinery to determine trace likelihood ratios. There is a threshold with value t1 =100. Traces with first stage likelihood ratios greater than 100 are passed to the second stage. The second stage uses only content (T) features and compares the T features of the test picture to the T features in each activated trace, as in bREM.
B. Context feature types. There could be a wide variety of context features for each stage and each condition in the present studies, but doing so would have added too many parameters and processes to explain what the data show, and to justify the increased complexity. Thus we distinguish explicitly two types of context features: context that changes during the first ten lists, termed ‘changing context’, or CC, and context that does not change during the first ten lists, termed ‘unchanging context’, or UC. All pictures are represented with 25 content features (T = 25), 28 changing context features (CC = 28), and 28 unchanging context features (UC = 28). In different conditions and studies not all these features are necessarily used for test. 
In order to retrieve, for both experiments in stage 1: During testing of the first ten lists, 13 of the 28 UC features are randomly selected and used with all 28 CC features to retrieve;  in final testing ??? of the UC features and ??? of the CC features are randomly selected and used to retrieve.
C. Context (and content) storage. During study in every condition of Experiment 1 every context and content feature is stored with the bREM parameters g = 0.3, c = 0.7, u = 0.4. During study in every condition of Experiment 2 every context and content feature is stored with the bREM parameters g =0.3, c = 0.8, u = 0.4. 
D. Context change. 1] CC does not change during Study of each list. CC changes could have been assumed during study of each initial list, thereby accounting for the serial position effects found in the data. However, we decided the added model complexity would not justify adding such assumptions, especially because the CC changes would simply be made to match the serial position effects observed. 
2] CC changes between study and test for each list. Each CC feature changes from its current value to a sample from the geometric distribution. The change occurs with probability δ_BST = 0.2396 (Exp. 1) and 0.307 (Exp. 2). The parameter value of the geometric distribution was g = 0.3. The many confusing foils in Exp. 2 justify the larger CC change.
3] CC and UC distortion and recovery during testing. The hit and false alarm data shown in Figures 2 and 7 show the performance is low for targets and high for foils for the first tests after a list and then recover as testing continues. In addition, final testing shows that both the first tested targets and foils have low performance for the first tests in initial lists, and then both improve as initial testing continues. This set of results is extraordinarily difficult to model or explain, and certainly requires a process other than simple CC change. To be precise, let the shift in CC features from study to test (as discussed in 2] above) produce a set of CC features termed CC*. The critical assumption: The shift from study to test causes a distortion of the CC and UC features used to probe memory, a distortion that gradually dissipates as testing continues. Thus let the CC* features and also the UC features be changed (distorted) by a probability ??? exp1 exp2. With these probabilities the then current feature value changes to a random selection from the base rate distribution. Then at each successive test the then current value of each feature reverts back to the value prior to the distortion with probability ???exp1 exp2.  As described in the next section, each test causes storage of a new test trace. The feature values stored in that new test trace are those present at the time of the test. Thus the feature values stored improve as the tests continue.
4. CC changes between tests of a list and study of the next list. Each CC feature changes from its current value to a sample from the geometric distribution (again with g = 0.3). The change probability is δ_BL = 0.456 (common to both experiments). 
5. CC changes  between initial lists and the final test. Following completion of all ten lists, each CC feature changed from its current value to a sample from the geometric (g = 0.3). The probability of change is δ_BIF = 0.08 (Exp. 1) and 0.18 (Exp. 2) .

7] ‘Unchanging’ context changes between the initial ten lists and final testing. Each UC feature changes from its current value to a sample from the geometric (g = 0.3). The probability of change is δ_BIF = 0.08 (Exp. 1) and 0.18 (Exp. 2). Unchanging refers only to the initial set of lists;  no context features remain unchanged permanently. UC changed every 42 tests from the current value to a choice from the geometric (again with g = 0.3). The probability of change is δ_BF = 0.02 (common to both experiments).
Storage of Traces During Testing.
Testing an picture is an event and like all events gets stored. Every test event should therefore produce storage, storage of both content and context features. We assume that each study or test event for a picture produces a trace stored as in bREM, with parameters u = 0.4; c = 0.85 (Exp. 1), 0.70 (Exp. 2); g = 0.3. Thus:: 
a] study of a picture without any subsequent tests produces a single study trace; 
b] test of a picture not studied (a new foil) produces a single test trace;
c) study of a picture tested on the same list, but not tested again, produces two traces, one for study, one for test;
d] study of a picture not tested on that same list, but then presented as a foil on the next list has two traces stored, one for study, one for test. 
e) test of a picture as a foil, and then tested again as a foil on the next list, has two traces stored, one for each test; 
f) study of a picture that is tested on that same list and then tested as a foil on the next list has three traces stored, one for study and one for each test.
These storage assumptions superficially deviate from previous modeling of list-strength effects by Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997, 1998) who argued that new traces were not stored most of the time when a word was studied several times on the same list. However, we are now discussing storage of test traces, a rather different process.  What is the amount of storage during testing is an open question answered in part by a tested picture’s performance in final testing. For example, the results show that final test performance for pictures studied but not tested was about equal to final test performance for pictures tested but not studied. This suggests that storage was about equal for study and test. 
Storage of test traces affects future performance because some of those traces pass stage 1 filtering, whenever their trace likelihood ratio exceeds 100. A trace tends to pass this activation threshold when the CC and UC features used for retrieval match those stored in the trace. The additional traces that are activated take part in stage two, using their content features. The number of additional traces activated tends to increase as testing continues due to the storage of test traces. The net effect is like that caused by an increase in list-length: By itself this factor tends to cause a performance decrease as testing continues, hence OI. This factor tends not to be very large, however, especially when CC change reduces activation of traces of pictures from prior lists. It turns out that a different factor may be as important, or more important, in producing OI: the result of strengthening of existing traces, as discussed next.  
Strengthening Existing Traces. 
As discussed by Shiffrin and Steyvers (1997), strengthening existing traces during study was essential to allow REM to predict list-strength effects. The idea explained briefly: When an item is studied additional times (massed or spaced), its trace tends to be sampled in a recall process that operates in parallel with computation of odds. When a trace is sampled it is strengthened by making its features better match the current test presentation. That produces differentiation – makes the trace better stored, but critically makes it less similar to other items to be tested later, thereby improving recognition of those items that are tested later.  
Similar processes operate during testing. When pictures are repeatedly tested, REM familiarity (odds)  is calculated, and in parallel a trace is sampled from those currently activated. The probability of sampling a trace is the ratio of its likelihood ratio to the sum of likelihood ratios of all activated traces. Sampling tends to produce choice of the strongest trace, so when a target is tested, its trace is often the one sampled. If the sampled trace has a likelihood value greater than a recall threshold of tr = 0.2 (Exp. 1)  and 0.3 (Exp. 2) , we assume that the participant makes the  default assumption that the trace sampled is indeed that of the test picture, and therefore strengthens it by filling in trace features found in the test picture that are missing from the trace, and by correcting trace features that do not match those in the test picture. To be specific, a missing feature in the trace and a trace feature not matching the test picture is replaced by the feature in the test picture with the usual storage rules, based on u, c, and g. 
Strengthening a trace has the direct effect of improving later retrieval when a later test of a target causes sampling of that trace. However, strengthening has two opposing effects on tests of other pictures tested later: 
1] The first effect is differentiation: In most cases, strengthening a trace makes it less similar to other stored traces# [footnote: Although not relevant for the present studies, differentiation will not occur when the picture tested later is highly similar to the one whose trace was strengthened. This could occur, for example, if a picture of a beach scene is strengthened and later that picture is tested with a slightly different wave approaching the beach.] A later test of some other picture will therefore match the strengthened trace less well, reducing that trace’s likelihood ratio, and reducing familiarity [odds]. The net result will be an increase in accuracy for a future test of a different picture. This process by itself produces a result opposite of OI, although this effect tends not to be large in magnitude.
2] There is a different effect of strengthening that in many cases will produce an increase in OI. In many studies there are a good number of false alarms when a foil is tested. When a false alarm occurs to a new foil not seen earlier in the experiment, the trace sampled cannot be the one produced by the test picture. Instead the sampled trace will be that of some picture seen earlier, often a target that has yet to be tested. When that target trace is sampled, adding to it the features of the foil currently being tested will degrade its quality. As a result, when the picture that did produce that trace is later tested its hit probability will drop, and OI will increase. In a typical recognition study with foils tested half the time, and with a good number of false alarms, there can be a net effect of output interference [OI]. This results from a balance of 1) increasing performance over testing due to differentiation and 2) decreasing performance over testing due to storage of test traces and due to strengthening of wrong traces when foils are tested. Factor 2) can dominate factor 1).
The Use of Recall in Recognition
The use of recall to help make recognition decisions has been studied and modeled for many years, as discussed in the introduction.  However, until the present modeling, recall in REM has only affected decision making indirectly, by strengthening of traces; each decision has been based on familiarity (the odds). REM has worked well using odds alone for decision due to the design of typical studies: Foils are pictures not seen earlier in the experiment. In such designs (like the present Exp.1), any picture reasonably familiar is a target. Because an picture with high familiarity will produce a trace likely to be recalled, a decision based on familiarity will tend to be the same as one produced by examination of a recalled trace. Thus the predictions made by the two decision processes are highly correlated, and use of familiarity alone suffices. Thus our modeling for Experiment 1 assumes as usual that decisions are made on the basis of familiarity.
There is nonetheless a long history of studies making a strong case that recall plays an important role in recognizing, even in standard experimental designs. The results of the present Experiment 2 makes this case even more strongly: In Experiment 2, three quarters of the foils are pictures seen on the prior list. This causes many foils to have high familiarity, and a decision based on familiarity would produce an old response (an error, a false alarm). Indeed the data shows these confusing foils to produce many errors. Critically for decisions made with familiarity, these errors would occur mostly for the pictures producing the strongest traces – the pictures studied and tested on the prior trial. Yet those pictures did not produce the greatest number of errors – decisions by familiarity predict the wrong ordering of false alarms across the conditions. Thus to model the observed  results, we assume, as in previous REM modeling, that recall of a trace occurs in parallel with computation of familiarity. In addition, we assume that the recalled trace may contain information that the trace was produced by appearance on a list other than the current list (i.e. the trace has information suggesting its list of origin). When information is found in a recalled trace that its origin was not the current list, then a decision can be made to respond new despite the high familiarity of the test picture. This is the process known as ‘recall to reject’. Such a process can explain the ordering of false alarms if the strongest traces contain the best origin information.
The existence of origin information in a trace should vary with the history of the picture used to produce that trace. E.g. A picture studied and tested should produce a trace with better  origin information than the trace of a picture studied and not tested. We assume the origin information stored in traces varies as just mentioned, but is noisy and sometimes wrong. Given the existence of this noise we assume that the origin information is not always used when found in a trace. Thus in Experiment 2 we assume the probability of using origin information to reject a familiar test picture grows as lists continue. That growth is a result of the participants adapting  their coding and retrieval to the presence of so many confusing foils from the prior list.
To implement these processes in the modeling, we assume that starting on list 2 there exist the following probabilities that a recalled trace will have correct origin information (if the origin information is not correct it is present but incorrect): A] ku = 0.25 for a trace produced by a picture studied on the current list but not seen on a prior list; B]  ks = 0.4 for a trace produced by a picture studied on the prior list, then tested as a foil on the current list; C] kt = 0.2 for a trace produced by a picture not studied but tested on the prior list and then tested as a foil on the current list; D] kb = 0.2 for a trace produced by a picture studied and tested on the prior list and then tested as a foil on the current list. 
Because the origin information is sometimes incorrect the participant is not always willing  to use it to govern responses in place of familiarity. Let the probability that it is used on list 2 be Z = 0.35. Let this probability rise as each list occurs: for j >1, let Z(j) = 1- [1-Z] Rj-2 . R = 0.9 gives the increase in willingness to use origin information to reject as lists continue. 
Note that use of origin information for a test of a current target would lead to a correct old response if the origin information is correct, but an incorrect new response if the origin information is wrong. Consider list 2. If a target is tested, with probability Z the decision will switch from use of familiarity to use of origin information; this will produce a correct response with probability ku = 0.25. However, using familiarity would produce a correct response with a probability about .92 ??? as seen on list 1. Thus the switch to use of origin information will reduce performance: hits will drop. Nonetheless the design of Experiment 2 provides a good reason for switching: Doing so will improve performance for the many confusing foils that are tested. These tend to have high familiarity and use of familiarity to decide would produce false alarms. Thus there is a trade-off: hits are sacrificed in order to reduce false alarms. As lists continue, and origin information is used increasingly often, both hits and false alarms for confusing foils will decrease (equivalently correct rejections will increase).

Assessment of the Model Predictions
        The predictions produced by the assumptions and parameter values outlined in the prior section have been shown in each of the figures, along with the observed data. The predictions are in good qualitative agreement with the observations, with one exception shown in Figure 3 for Experiment 1 – we did not find any plausible way to add assumptions that would keep the CRs flat while maintaining the good predictions for all the other conditions. 
The parameter values have not been optimized but rather tuned partly by hand to produce this degree of agreement with the data that is shown. As argued previously, the data is a complex mixture produced by differences in participants, pictures, and much else, so it does not make sense to produce a best quantitative fit for a model that does not incorporate all the mixture components. 
The data from the many conditions of these two studies was extraordinarily complex, and in many instances required starting with the fundamental assumptions of the REM model and layering on top of them assumptions tuned to produce the form of the observed data. This approach can be criticized for its post-hoc nature, but it seems certain that decent predictions, even qualitatively accurate predictions, could not have been obtained in any other way. As a partial response to such criticisms, an attempt was made in the prior section to show that assumptions  may have been produced after viewing the data, but were sensible and reasonably well justified. 
        One might well ask whether other models could have been used to produce an equally good account of the results. The answer is surely yes, though it seems likely such alternative models would have had to adopt assumptions very similar to those used to produce this augmentation of REM. There being an uncountable number of such alternative models and the task of producing even the present one having proved difficult and daunting, we leave alternative models for future research.
Summary and Conclusion
        The studies in this research were designed to shed light on certain processes important for recognizing, and to model those processes by building upon the very successful REM modeling framework. The studies used several novel designs to do this: 1) Final recognition testing was carried out in which every item seen in the first ten study and test lists was defined as a target  requiring an old response. 2) Experiment 1 assessed the possibility that participants could use knowledge of the storage context to improve retrieval in final testing by informing them of the list on which the current set of targets was experienced. 3) Experiment 2 used many foils on the current test list (foils because they were not studied on the current list) that were seen on the prior list. This manipulation was designed to make such foils so familiar that use of familiarity for recognizing would fail badly, and therefore motivate the participants to use recall to reject otherwise highly familiar test pictures. These designs produced a rich set of results that led to the formulation of an augmented REM model to explain the causes of the findings.
        The resultant REM model was complex with many detailed assumptions, assumptions that could easily lead the reader to mistake the trees for the forest. Thus it is useful to list here the core processes that have been used to augment REM. We suspect that most or all of these processes would be needed to account for the main results in any other modeling framework.
Context features that help identify those traces that were stored in a given situation.
Context features of different types. 
Context features that change at different rates and at different times.
Storage of test traces as well as study traces.
Strengthening of traces sampled during recognition testing.
Use of trace recall to reject highly familiar pictures.
These assumptions and processes were layered on top of the core assumptions of REM (termed bREM): items represented as vectors of features and feature values; items stored in memory as separate traces; traces formed at storage as incomplete and noisy (error prone) vectors; retrieval due to matching test vectors to each memory trace, in parallel; the matching based on similarity of the test vector to each trace vector, producing a likelihood ratio for each trace giving the probability that the trace was produced by study of the test item vs. study of some other item; the recognition decision based on familiarity defined as the average of the trace likelihood ratios, known as the ‘odds’ that the test item had been studied; the decision being to respond old if the odds was greater than 1, and respond new otherwise. Perhaps the most important of these assumptions, the one that helps bREM to predict the main patterns of recognition results with the same parameter values and decision criterion for all conditions, is this: the assumption that the recognition decision is based on a ratio of an item strength based on the assumption that the test item was studied to an item strength based on the assumption that the test item was not studied, This assumption lies at the core of the present modeling, though partially modified to predict a few of the findings.
        The present modeling used many other assumptions and processes that should be considered ‘auxiliary’, auxiliary in the sense that they are assumptions needed to account for the detailed patterns of the data. It is never easy to distinguish core assumptions from auxiliary assumptions, but the best justification for the present distinction is the reliance on the basic assumptions of bREM to build the model. 
        It is important to note that both bREM and augmented REM used to explain the present data are causal, mechanistic models. They explain the cause and source of the ‘strengths’ used to make recognition decisions. Such modeling can be distinguished from modeling used to describe the patterns of data rather than their causes, This is important to emphasize because it has been traditional to analyze recognition results with signal detection modeling (SDT): It is assumed that for each condition decisions are based on a sample of a strength from a distribution of target strengths when a target is tested, and a sample from a (smaller) distribution of strengths when a foil is tested. The two distributions are traditionally gaussians, with target mean and variance larger than the foil mean and variance. For a given condition a criterion is chosen and an old response is given if the test item’s strength is above that criterion. REM in its different forms is also a signal detection model: It has distributions of strengths for targets and foils and posits an old response if the test item strength is above a criterion. However, REM gives the causes of the strengths (causes that produce highly skewed distributions rather than symmetric gaussians), causes that produce the differences in the distributions for different conditions. Thus bREM is able to use the same parameter values and the same criterion for all conditions. That being noted, we do not argue that REM is a different and better model than SDT. REM has the same basic structure as an SDT model. it would not be sensible to pit the two modeling approaches against each other. They have different goals.

Retention of Slow Responders
Visual inspection of individual reaction time (RT) plots revealed several participants with notably slower responses (median RTs approximately 2–3 standard deviations above the sample mean). These participants were retained in the final analyses because they satisfied all pre-specified inclusion criteria and demonstrated consistent, accurate performance.
As specified in the main text, exclusions were based on median RTs rather than means, using a conservative ±3 SD cutoff. This approach minimizes the undue influence of a small number of unusually slow trials and better reflects participants’ typical response latencies. Under this criterion, participants with median RTs ≤ 3 SD from the sample mean were retained. All such individuals exhibited accuracy well above chance (range = 0.64–0.93), stable response patterns across trials, and no evidence of fatigue or disengagement. Retaining these participants preserves genuine individual differences in processing speed (e.g., more deliberate or cautious response styles) and avoids biasing the sample toward unusually fast responders.
One participant exhibited a median RT of approximately 1,858 ms (z = 6.21) during the final test, exceeding the nominal exclusion threshold. This participant nonetheless maintained high task accuracy (0.73) and displayed stable performance across blocks, suggesting a consistently slow but attentive response style rather than random or inattentive behavior. Sensitivity analyses conducted with and without this participant yielded no qualitative changes to the results or statistical conclusions.
In sum, all retained participants either met the pre-specified inclusion criteria (median RT ≤ 3 SD) or demonstrated performance indicative of genuine task engagement. Excluding these individuals would have introduced an unnecessary sampling bias, while their inclusion did not alter the overall pattern of findings.

Retention of Slow Responders (Experiment 3)
As in Experiment 1, visual inspection of reaction time (RT) distributions in Experiment 3 revealed several participants with slower response profiles (median RTs ≈ 1,200–1,400 ms).
