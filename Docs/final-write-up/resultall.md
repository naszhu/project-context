# Method

## Statistical Analysis

Recognition accuracy was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial error distribution and logit link function, implemented in R using the lme4 package. Position effects were modeled using orthogonal polynomial contrasts (linear and quadratic terms). All models included random intercepts for participants; initial test analyses also included uncorrelated random slopes for linear position trends, while final test analyses used random intercepts only due to convergence constraints. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = .05. Complete model specifications, convergence diagnostics, and detailed statistical results are provided in Appendix E. 






# Results of Experiment 1
## Data Exclusion and Quality Control
A total of 206 participants completed the experiment (backward: n = 50; forward: n = 53; random: n = 103). Data quality screening followed standard cognitive-psychology procedures (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) overall accuracy onr initial test performance > 2.5 SD below the sample mean, (b) median response time > 3 SD from the mean, (c) > 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, or (d) > 30% performance decline from initial to final test. Trials faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were also excluded.
Ten participants (4.9%) were removed for low accuracy or disengagement (e.g., rushed responding or extreme performance decline). One additional participant was retained despite extreme initial performance (z = –5.17) because accuracy improved to normal levels on the final test, response times were within the typical range, and the pattern reflected genuine learning rather than inattention.
The final sample included 196 participants (backward: 48; forward: 48; random: 100) contributing 126,876 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and received compensation consistent with institutional review board guidelines.
The main results will be displayed in figures as well as given as values in the appendices. The data are shown along with standard errors of the mean (across participants). 

## Analysis Overview

The following analyses examine recognition accuracy across different phases and position types. The interpretation of the results depends jointly on the way a given picture type is recognized when tested in the initial lists and the way that same item is recognized in the final testing, because the final test performance provides an indication of storage strength. Thus discussion of the meaning of the findings is in most cases deferred either until final test results are presented, or until the model is presented.

**Initial Test Analyses:** (1) Study position effects for targets only (linear and quadratic terms); (2) Test position effects with item type (target vs. foil) interactions; (3) Between-list effects with item type interactions.

**Final Test Analyses:** (1) Study position effects for items with study history (ST and SO); (2) Test position effects for items with initial test history (ST and TO); (3) Combined order analysis examining both final test order and initial list order across three presentation conditions (forward, backward, random) with all item types.

Detailed model specifications and statistical results are provided in Appendix E.
## Organization of Results
The results are organized as follows: For each experiment, we first examine within-list effects during initial testing (study position and test position effects), followed by between-list effects during initial testing. We then examine final test performance as a function of original study and test positions from the initial lists, followed by final test performance as a function of list order (both final test order and initial list order).

 

## Initial Within-List Study Position Effects

Study position often shows serial position effects, as did the present study, as depicted in Figure 7.1.a, left panel. Such effects have been modeled many times (e.g. Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968). [These serial position effects are not a primary interest of the present investigations and will not be modeled. Instead the modeling will predict performance averaged across study position].

Recognition accuracy for targets declined significantly across study positions (linear trend: p < .001), with a significant positive quadratic component (p < .001) indicating a curved serial-position function. Accuracy was highest for the earliest positions, declined most sharply through the middle of the list, and levelled off (or partially recovered) at the end—an inverted-U pattern characteristic of simultaneous primacy and recency influences. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E. 
## Initial Within-List Test Position Effects

Test position effects are important results that help us understand features used to probe memory, and help us augment the bREM model. These test position effects are shown in Figure 7.1.b. CRs were considerably higher than hits (as is often found, and as is predicted by bREM). Hs increased strongly over tests. CRs decreased less strongly over tests. Overall performance increased slightly over tests. Because overall performance almost remains constant, at least some of much of the increase in Hs and decrease in CRs may be due to a shift in decision criterion as lists continue. But note that our modeling identifies a number of other factors also operating to produce test position effects. These results are important in part because they show the opposite of output interference, and in part because they suggest a distortion of retrieval at the start of testing following each of the initial lists (see later discussion in the modeling section).

The Test Position × Picture Type interaction showed sharply diverging patterns: foil rejection declined substantially across test positions (linear trend: p < .001), while target recognition increased (linear trend: p < .001). ST pictures showed substantially lower overall recognition accuracy than TO pictures (p < .001), with estimated marginal means of approximately 90.4% for targets versus 95.6% for foils. Overall accuracy improved modestly (~2%) when picture types were collapsed, rising from 88.3% (positions 1–5) to 90.2% (9–12), but this gain reflected opposing effects: ST improved while TO declined. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Initial Between-List Effects

Across lists we see clear evidence of output interference, as shown in Figure 8.a. Overall performance drops due to a strong decrease in Hs (green). CRs (orange) are largely stable over lists.

For TO pictures, correct rejection rates remained stable across lists (linear trend: p = .835), with a significant positive quadratic trend (p < .001) indicating a reliable non-linear deviation. Visual inspection suggests this reflects a shallow mid-list dip rather than a pronounced inverted-U pattern. ST pictures showed substantially lower overall recognition accuracy than TO pictures (p < .001), averaging 86.2% correct recognition (range: 82.6%–94.2%) versus 93.6% for foils (range: 92.2%–94.2%). The strong negative interaction between List Number and Picture Type (p < .001) shows that ST recognition significantly decreased from early to late lists (94.2% in list 1 to 82.6% in list 7), while TO recognition remained stable. The quadratic interaction (p < .001) revealed stronger primacy and recency effects for ST pictures. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E. 
## Final Within-List Study Position Effects

Figure 9.1.a.b shows that study position shows little effect of initial study position. This result is notably different from the serial study position effects observed during study of the initial lists. This result suggests that the initial list study position effects were not due to changes in trace strength, or they would have appeared in final testing. Forgetting did take place between initial lists and final testing, but it is hard to imagine forgetting that would only affect the primacy and recency portions of the study lists.

Across pictures, the linear trend for SO items was modest and did not reach significance (p = .065), indicating only a slight tendency for accuracy to dip at later study positions. A reliable positive quadratic component remained (p < .001), reflecting the familiar primacy–recency bend. ST pictures showed much higher recognition accuracy than SO pictures (p < .001), with estimated marginal means of approximately 68.2% for SO and 90.0% for ST, underscoring a robust testing effect. The Study Position × Picture Type interaction was nonsignificant (p = .730), indicating similar linear trends for SO and ST items across study positions. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.  

## Final Within-List Test Position Effects

Final test accuracy strongly increased with initial list test position for both targets and foils, suggesting initial list test position effects were due to trace strength differences. The increase in foils was opposite the initial list decrease. There is no simple way to explain these test position effects. The increase for both initial foils and initial targets suggests some sort of distortion at the start of testing that dissipates as testing continues.

Across pictures, a strong positive linear trend persisted (p < .001), indicating that recognition continued to improve for items tested later within the initial list. A negative quadratic component (p = .001) captured the slowing of this improvement toward the end of the list. TO pictures showed substantially lower recognition accuracy than ST pictures (p < .001), with observed means of 64.2% versus 88.2%, demonstrating that full initial exposure (study plus test) provides substantially greater benefit than partial exposure (test only). The Linear × Picture Type and quadratic interactions were nonsignificant (p = .233 and p = .359, respectively), indicating that TO and ST pictures climbed at comparable rates with similar curvature. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Final Between-List Effects by Final Test Order and Initial List Order

Note that the backward and forward results are mirrors of each other measured by input list position or final test position. Thus in Figure 10, the first and second columns of row 1 show the same data reversed, and the first and second columns of row 2 show the same data.

Figure 10.1.a shows output interference (OI) for all picture types. The final test order manipulation (forward, backward, random) showed effects important for modeling. A] Initial targets have the highest final Hs, suggesting they were stored strongly; there is little OI for these pictures, possibly due to ceiling effects. B] Pictures studied only and pictures tested only had much lower Hs and differed little from each other suggesting they had been stored much more weakly than targets (i.e studied and tested pictures) but were stored with similar strengths. C] The backward condition showed strong recency, and strong OI compared to the other conditions. It is assumed that this is due to two factors acting in concert: 1] use of enough changing context in final test retrieval to produce recency – better performance for the most recent lists seen; 2] output interference (OI) that causes performance to decrease as testing continues (see the modeling section for a discussion of the factors causing OI). D] the backward condition also showed some primacy. The best account is a gradual reconstruction of changing context as the testing moves to earlier lists. This idea will be used to explain the forward data. E] The forward condition results show greatly reduced performance as lists continue. Our best account is reconstruction of changing context. E.g. When the participants are told that the targets are coming from the first list, they manage to reconstruct context that is more similar to that stored originally than the current context. This better context continues to be used as testing continues. F] The random condition provides feedback that tells the participant what list a target came from, but that list randomly jumps among lists as testing continues. It is unclear under these circumstances whether any reconstruction of context would occur. Thus in the random condition it is assumed that the context used at the start of final testing is that after the change from initial lists to final testing. (Looking ahead to the modeling, it should be mentioned that an account for the large output interference observed, as seen most unambiguously in the random condition, requires changes in context regularly as final testing proceeds).

Different item types showed substantially different overall recognition accuracy: ST items were markedly more accurate than FTO (p < .001, ≈88.9% vs. ≈83.7%), while SO and TO items performed worse than FTO (≈66.5% and ≈63.8%, respectively; both p < .001). Overall accuracy did not differ by condition (all p > .60), confirming that presentation order modulated list-position patterns but not baseline performance. For the forward condition with FTO items, robust output interference emerged (linear trend: p < .001). Backward testing produced a far steeper decline (p < .001), while the random condition's decline was intermediate. When list order was expressed by initial position, slopes reversed strongly (p < .001), underscoring the divergence between test order and original study order. All item types displayed stronger positive curvature than FTO (all p < .001), with ST items showing the most pronounced curvature. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
 


# Results of Experiment 2
## Data Exclusion and Quality Control
A total of 199 participants completed the experiment through online recruitment via Prolific. Data quality screening was conducted using multiple exclusion criteria following standard practices in cognitive psychology research (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). At the participant level, individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) below-chance performance (accuracy < 50%), indicating random responding; (b) overall task accuracy more than 2.5 standard deviations below the sample mean; (c) median response time more than 3 standard deviations from the sample mean, indicating atypical response patterns; or (d) more than 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, indicating insufficient processing time or rushed responding. At the trial level, responses faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were excluded from all analyses, consistent with established response window parameters.
Thirteen participants (6.5%) were excluded based on participant-level criteria. Specifically, nine participants exhibited below-chance performance (M = 0.48, SD = 0.01, range = 0.48–0.50), indicating they were responding randomly or not engaging meaningfully with the task. Two additional participants exhibited extremely low accuracy (22.9% and 29.1%, both >3 SD below the mean) combined with below-chance performance. Three participants were excluded due to extreme response time outliers (median RTs of 281 ms, 1,654 ms, and 1,669 ms; |z| > 3.3), with one also exhibiting rushed responding (19.5% of responses < 200 ms). At the trial level, 479 trials (0.31% of all test trials) were excluded due to response times outside the 150–3,500 ms window (355 trials < 150 ms, 124 trials > 3,500 ms).
The final sample consisted of 186 participants contributing 156,214 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated for their time in accordance with institutional review board guidelines.
## Analysis Overview

The following analyses parallel those from Experiment 1, with additional item type categories to account for confusing foils (items appearing across consecutive lists):

**Initial Test Analyses:** (1) Study position effects for targets and confusing foils with defined study positions (ST, SO(n), ST(n)); (2) Test position effects with multiple item types including confusing foils; (3) Between-list effects with multiple item types.

**Final Test Analyses:** (1) Study position effects for items with study history; (2) Test position effects for items with initial test history; (3) Final test position effects (binned into 10 temporal groups) to assess output interference during final testing; (4) Initial list order effects for items with initial exposure history.

All within-list position effects were analyzed over 30 serial positions but plotted in 10 equally spaced bins for visual clarity. Detailed model specifications and statistical results are provided in Appendix E.
## Initial Within-List Study Position Effects

Figure 12.1.a shows the existence of serial position effects, as was found in Exp 1. As was the case for Exp 1, these effects were not modeled.

ST items served as the reference category (≈78% accuracy). SO(n) responses were 1.07 logits lower than targets (p < .001, ≈55% accuracy), and ST(n) responses were 0.85 logits lower (p < .001, ≈61% accuracy). A strong positive linear trend emerged for ST items (p < .001), indicating that recognition accuracy increased across study positions. For SO(n) items, the linear trend was negative but nonsignificant (p > .05), suggesting little systematic change. A significant positive quadratic trend appeared for ST items (p < .001), reflecting higher accuracy at later positions. SO(n) items showed a significant negative quadratic effect (p < .001), indicating poorer performance toward list ends. The study-position effect differed markedly by item type, with SO(n) and ST(n) items showing much shallower linear slopes than ST items (both p < .001). Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Initial Within-List Test Position Effects

Figure 12.1.b shows test position effects conditioned on the various types of test pictures. The solid lines show current list target Hs and current list foil CRs. These show roughly the pattern of Experiment 1, with Hs rising and CRs falling as tests continue. The explanation for these results from both studies will be presented in the modeling presentation.

The most interesting results are in the dashed lines that show correct rejections (CRs) for current list foils that had been seen on the prior trial (termed 'confusing foils'). Due to their presence on the prior trial these test pictures are highly familiar and as a result are given many false alarms (FAs), or equivalently are given low CRs. The most familiar pictures are those studied and tested on the prior trial (as demonstrated by final test results to be shown shortly). If familiarity (REM odds, or some other measure of degree of familiarity) were used to respond, these pictures would be given the greatest false alarms (FAs), and equivalently the lowest CRs. Yet these very familiar pictures produce CRs higher than pictures from the prior trial that were studied only, and not much lower than CRs for foils seen on the prior trial. Such results suggest that participants faced with highly familiar pictures that are not from the current list start using a recall process: They recall a trace likely to be one produced by the test picture and assess the trace contents. When they find information in that trace that identifies the origin of the picture producing the trace to be the prior list, they ignore the high familiarity and respond new. This is a process known as 'recall to reject' (e.g. Rotello, Macmillan & Van Tassel, 2000). This process is described in detail in the modeling section.

Different picture types showed substantially different overall recognition accuracy: TO pictures showed the highest accuracy (≈82.0%), followed by ST pictures (≈78.9%), while STnST(n) (≈61.1%) and SOnSO(n) (≈55.7%) showed lower accuracy. Test position slopes varied dramatically by picture type: ST pictures showed a large positive slope (p < .001), indicating that target recognition improved as testing progressed, while SO(n) showed a steep negative slope (p < .001), indicating severe output interference for these confusing foils. ST pictures also showed a strong negative quadratic interaction (p < .001), indicating a U-shaped pattern. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Initial Between-List Effects

Figure 13 shows between list results during initial lists, again conditioned on type of test picture. The relatively constant CRs for current list foils (new foils, not confusing foils) suggests that the decision threshold based on familiarity is changing little if at all as lists continue.

Each of the three types of confusing foils shows good improvement in CRs as lists continue, suggesting a gradual adjustment to the large number of confusing foils. In addition, current list targets show a decrease in Hs. In the modeling section we show how the increase in CRs and the decrease in Hs are both a natural result of a switch to the use of recall to reject.

Item types differed sharply in overall accuracy: Regular new foils (TO) were far easier to reject (≈81.8% CRs) than confusing foils that were foils on the prior list (TO(n), ≈66.5% CRs). Current-list targets (ST) were recognized well (≈79.1% hits), while ST(n) (≈58.9%) and SO(n) (≈54.1%) showed depressed accuracy. For TO(n) foils, accuracy improved markedly across successive lists (p < .001), indicating that participants rapidly adapted to the prevalence of confusing foils. ST(n) climbed even more steeply (p < .001), whereas SO(n) rose somewhat less (p = .005). In contrast, current-list items declined: TO new foils and ST targets both dropped sharply (both p < .001). These patterns reflect simultaneous gains in rejecting confusing foils and growing output interference for current-list items. Together, these results reveal a pronounced shift in decision dynamics across lists: participants rapidly adopted a "recall to reject" strategy for confusing foils, while current-list targets bore the brunt of the trade-off. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Final Within-List Study Position Effects

Figure 14.1.a.b shows that final test pictures give higher hits in final testing the more times they are seen during the initial lists, when analyzed either by study or test position within the initial lists. There is little effect of list study. There is a modest increase in performance as a function of initial list test position, albeit a smaller increase than in Exp. 1.

As was the case for Exp. 1, the absence of study position effects in final testing suggests that the serial position effects seen in initial testing were due to some sort of local context effects that are temporary. Given that the study position effects in initial testing did not persist to final testing, we decided not to include them in the modeling: They were not judged important enough to justify the additional complexity in the model.

For the reference item type SO(n) (≈63.6% accuracy), a modest negative linear trend emerged (p = .015), indicating that recognition accuracy dropped slightly for items studied later within each list. A positive quadratic component remained (p < .001), reflecting a gentle primacy–recency bend. Relative to the reference, SO(n) pictures showed higher accuracy (≈82.4%), ST pictures were even more accurate (≈84.4%), and ST(n) pictures achieved the highest accuracy (≈91.3%; all p < .001). Comparing items with matched exposure histories, ST pictures (studied and tested once) outperformed the reference single-study baseline, and ST(n) pictures (retested as confusing foils) added further benefit, highlighting the cumulative benefit of repeated retrieval. The study-position decline was slightly more negative for SOn and slightly more positive for ST and ST(n), though these contrasts did not reach conventional significance (all p > .05). Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Final Within-List Test Position Effects

The within list test position effects shown in Figure 14 for targets and foils (targets increasing and foils decreasing across the test positions) do not appear in final testing. The increases in hits seen in Exp. 1 are very modest in Exp. 2, if present at all. These results are a challenge for explanations and modeling. Further discussion is deferred to the section presenting the model.

ST pictures showed a robust positive linear trend (p < .001, ≈84.4% accuracy). ST(n) pictures exhibited a smaller, non-significant increase across test positions (p = .123), yet retained the highest marginal mean (≈91.3% accuracy). TO, TO(n), and SO(n) pictures all showed significant positive trends (p < .05), with marginal means ranging from ≈59.2% to ≈83.3%. ST pictures showed a modest negative quadratic component (p = .042), indicating a gentle downward bend for items tested near list endpoints. Relative to ST pictures, ST(n) slopes were markedly flatter (p = .006), and TO and SO(n) slopes were also reduced (p = .028 and p = .071, respectively), underscoring that ST items benefitted most consistently from later test positions. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.

## Final Between-List Effects by Final Test Position

Figure 15.1.a shows strong evidence of output interference (OI). This finding is consistent with that from the random test condition of Exp. 1, and with the various demonstrations of strong OI by Criss, Malmberg. Shiffrin and others (discussed in the introduction) for recognition study followed by a sufficiently long series of tests. The accuracy of the different picture types generally follows the pattern seen in Exp. 1: Higher accuracy for pictures seen more times in the initial ten lists.

Different picture types showed substantially different overall recognition accuracy compared to FTO (≈74.4% accuracy): STnST(n) pictures showed the highest accuracy (≈90.5%), followed by ST pictures (≈83.5%), SOnSO(n) (≈81.6%), and TOnTO(n) (≈77.7%), while SO (≈63.1%) and TO (≈59.1%) showed lower accuracy (all p < .001). For FTO, a substantial negative linear trend emerged (p < .001), indicating that recognition accuracy declined systematically as the final test progressed. A significant positive quadratic trend emerged across all picture types (p = .008), indicating a modest inverted-U pattern. The Final Test Position × Picture Type interaction revealed divergent output interference patterns: SOnSO(n) and TOnTO(n) pictures showed large negative interactions (both p < .001), yielding steeper combined negative trends than FTO. ST, TO, and STnST(n) pictures showed moderate negative interactions (all p < .001), while SO pictures showed a positive interaction (p < .001), yielding a less steep combined negative trend, suggesting reduced vulnerability to output interference compared to other picture types. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
## Final Between-List Effects by Initial List Order

Figure 15.1.b shows final test accuracy as a function of the initial list position. The results are similar to the Exp. 1 results shown in Figure 15.1.b for the random test group. There is some slight recency (higher accuracy for pictures seen on the more recent lists).

Relative to TO(n) (≈80.1% accuracy), TO items were less accurate (≈59.7%), SO items also underperformed (≈63.3%), while SO(n) (≈83.8%), ST (≈84.1%), and ST(n) (≈91.7%) items exceeded the baseline (all p < .001). Items repeated across lists (SO(n), ST(n), TO(n)) averaged ≈86.5%, outperforming single-list items (SO, ST, TO; ≈70.4%) by about 16.1 percentage points, highlighting a strong repeated-exposure benefit. TO(n) displayed a large positive slope (p < .001). TO showed an even steeper rise (p < .001), whereas SO, SO(n), ST, and ST(n) retained positive, though varied, slopes—indicating better retention for items from later initial lists across all types. A significant positive quadratic trend emerged across all picture types (p < .001), indicating a reliable inverted-U pattern, reflecting primacy and recency deficits in long-term retention. Relative to the TO(n) baseline, TO showed a strong positive interaction (p < .001), while SO and SO(n) displayed significant negative interactions (both p < .001), and ST and ST(n) interactions were small and nonsignificant. Together, these contrasts indicate that repeated-exposure foils (TO, TO(n)) gained the most from later lists, while studied-only items (SO, SO(n)) benefited less, and ST/ST(n) items maintained robust positive trends regardless of list position. Complete statistical details are provided in Appendix E.
