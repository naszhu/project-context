# Method

## Statistical Analysis

### Model Specification 
Recognition accuracy was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial error distribution and logit link function, implemented in R (Version 4.3.1; R Core Team, 2024) using the lme4 package (Version 1.1-35.1; Bates et al., 2015). All models were fitted using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno quasi-Newton optimization algorithm (BOBYQA; Powell, 2009). Statistical significance was evaluated at α = .05. Item-type-specific trends were estimated using the emmeans package (Version 1.8.9; Lenth, 2023), and confidence intervals were computed using the Wald method.
### Fixed and Random Effects
Position effects were modeled using orthogonal polynomial contrasts (linear and quadratic terms) to capture both monotonic trends and curvilinear patterns across serial positions. Fixed effects included position terms (study position, test position, or list number, depending on the analysis) fully crossed with item type (e.g., target (ST) vs. foil (TO) for initial tests; ST vs. SO vs. TO vs. FTO for final tests). For the final test order analysis in Experiment 1, an additional ordering-type factor distinguished effects by final test position from effects by initial list position.
All models included random intercepts for participants to account for individual differences in overall performance. Following the principle of maximal random effects justified by design (Barr et al., 2013), we attempted to include random slopes for position effects whenever possible. For initial test analyses (study position, test position, and between-list effects), we successfully included uncorrelated random slopes for linear position trends. However, for all final test analyses, models with random slopes failed to converge despite extended iterations and alternative optimizers, requiring us to use random intercepts only. When models failed to converge, we simplified systematically by removing correlations between random slopes, then removing random slopes for quadratic terms, then linear terms, and finally retaining only random intercepts if necessary. For the final test combined order analysis (three conditions × two ordering types × four item types), the large number of fixed effect parameters required random intercepts only. All fixed effects were retained exactly as specified by the experimental design and theoretical framework, regardless of random effects simplifications. 
###  Experiment-Specific Design Considerations 
Final Test Position Binning. Final test positions were binned into 10 temporal bins (1–8 with 49 items each, 9–10 with 50 items each) for Experiment 2 to create sufficient observations per bin while maintaining temporal resolution.
Reference Category Selection. Because FTO items have no list number variation, STnST(n) served as the reference category for slope comparisons in interaction terms, while FTO remained the intercept reference in Experiment 2 analyses. This dual-reference approach allows for meaningful interpretation of both intercept differences (using FTO as baseline) and slope differences (using STnST(n) as baseline for list number effects).
### Convergence and Model Diagnostics 
We checked convergence using two criteria: (1) relative gradient should be less than 0.001, and (2) the Hessian matrix should be positive definite. Most models converged very well (relative gradients between 0.0000047 and 0.0018). Two complex models had convergence warnings: the final test initial order analysis (0.022) and the combined order analysis (0.072). For these models, we checked that the results were stable by trying different optimization settings (up to 500,000 iterations), comparing results to simpler models, and using fallback methods when needed. All results represent the best models we could fit given the complexity of the designs. 






# Results of Experiment 1
## Data Exclusion and Quality Control
A total of 206 participants completed the experiment (backward: n = 50; forward: n = 53; random: n = 103). Data quality screening followed standard cognitive-psychology procedures (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) overall accuracy onr initial test performance > 2.5 SD below the sample mean, (b) median response time > 3 SD from the mean, (c) > 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, or (d) > 30% performance decline from initial to final test. Trials faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were also excluded.
Ten participants (4.9%) were removed for low accuracy or disengagement (e.g., rushed responding or extreme performance decline). One additional participant was retained despite extreme initial performance (z = –5.17) because accuracy improved to normal levels on the final test, response times were within the typical range, and the pattern reflected genuine learning rather than inattention.
The final sample included 196 participants (backward: 48; forward: 48; random: 100) contributing 126,876 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and received compensation consistent with institutional review board guidelines.
The main results will be displayed in figures as well as given as values in the appendices. The data are shown along with standard errors of the mean (across participants). 
Analysis Overview
The following analyses examine recognition accuracy across different phases and position types: The interpretation of the results depends jointly on the way a given picture type is recognized when tested in the initial lists and the way that same item is recognized in the final testing, because the final test performance provides an indication of storage strength. Thus discussion of the meaning of the findings is in most cases deferred either until final test results are presented, or until the model is presented.
Initial Test: Study Position Effects. Study position effects during initial testing were examined using a GLMM with accuracy as the dependent variable, study position (linear and quadratic orthogonal polynomial terms) and item type (target vs. foil) as fixed effects, and random intercepts and slopes for study position by participant. This analysis tested whether serial position effects during encoding influenced immediate recognition performance and whether these effects differed for target recognition versus foil rejection.
Initial Test: Test Position Effects. Test position effects during initial testing were examined to assess whether output interference accumulated during the test phase itself and whether such interference differed for target recognition versus foil rejection. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, test position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type (target vs. foil) as fixed effects, and random intercepts and slopes for test position by participant.
Initial Test: Between-List Effects. Between-list effects during initial testing were examined to assess whether proactive interference accumulated across successive study-test lists and whether interference patterns differed for target recognition versus foil rejection. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, list number (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant.
Final Test: Study Position Effects. For items with study history (ST and SO), final test recognition was examined as a function of original study position within each initial list. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, study position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant. This analysis tested whether within-list serial position effects established during encoding persisted into delayed recognition and whether retrieval practice during initial testing modified these patterns.
Final Test: Test Position Effects. For items with initial test history (ST and TO), final test recognition was examined as a function of original test position within each initial list. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, test position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant. This analysis tested whether within-list output interference effects observed during initial testing persisted into delayed recognition.
Final Test: Combined Order Effects (Primary Analysis). Recognition in the final test was examined using a combined list-order analysis that accounted for the relationship between initial and final test order across presentation conditions. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, list order (linear and quadratic terms), condition (forward, backward, random), ordering type (by final test order vs. by initial list order), and item type as fixed effects, with all theoretically relevant interactions, and random intercepts by participant. In the forward condition, initial order equals final order; in the backward condition, initial order is the mirror of final order (list 1 initial = list 10 final); in the random condition, initial and final orders are uncorrelated. An ordering-type factor was introduced to distinguish effects by final test order (present in all conditions) from effects by initial list order (present only in the random condition, analyzed as additional observations).
## Organization of Results
The results are organized as follows: For each experiment, we first examine within-list effects during initial testing (study position and test position effects), followed by between-list effects during initial testing. We then examine final test performance as a function of original study and test positions from the initial lists, followed by final test performance as a function of list order (both final test order and initial list order).

 

## Initial Within-List Study Position Effects
Study position often shows serial position effects, as did the present study, as depicted in Figure 7.1.a, left panel. Such effects have been modeled many times (e.g. Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968). [These serial position effects are not a primary interest of the present investigations and will not be modeled. Instead the modeling will predict performance averaged across study position]. 
Linear Trends Across Study Positions.  Recognition accuracy for targets (TO) declined significantly across study positions, b = -12.73, SE = 3.27, z = -3.90, p < .001, 95% CI [-19.13, -6.33]. Items studied later in the list were remembered less accurately than items presented near the start. 
Quadratic Trends Across Study Positions. A significant positive quadratic component emerged, b = 21.52, SE = 2.82, z = 7.62, p < .001, 95% CI [15.98, 27.05], indicating a curved serial-position function. Accuracy was highest for the earliest positions, declined most sharply through the middle of the list, and levelled off (or partially recovered) at the end—an inverted-U pattern characteristic of simultaneous primacy and recency influences. 
## Initial Within-List Test Position Effects
 Test position effects are important results that help us understand features used to probe memory, and help us augment the bREM model.These test position effects are shown in Figure 7.1.b. CRs were considerably higher than hits (as is often found, and as is predicted by bREM). Hs increased strongly over tests. CRs decreased less strongly over tests. Overall performance increased slightly over tests. Because overall performance almost remains constant, at least some of much of the increase in Hs and decrease in CRs may be due to a shift in decision criterion as lists continue. But note that our modeling identifies a number of other factors also operating to produce test position effects. These results are important in part because they show the opposite of output interference, and in part because they suggest a distortion of retrieval at the start of testing following each of the initial lists (see later discussion in the modeling section). 
Overall Patterns by Test Position. For TO pictures, a strong negative linear trend emerged, b = -40.96, SE = 2.58, z = -15.87, p < .001, 95% CI [-46.02, -35.90], indicating that correct rejection rates declined systematically and substantially across test positions. A modest positive quadratic trend also emerged for TO pictures, b = 9.05, SE = 3.52, z = 2.57, p = .010, 95% CI [2.15, 15.94], indicating a slight curvature superimposed on the linear decline.  
Picture Type Effects. ST pictures showed substantially lower overall recognition accuracy than TO pictures, b = -0.89, SE = 0.04, z = -24.18, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.96, -0.82]. The estimated marginal means were 3.07 on the logit scale for foils (corresponding to approximately 95.6% accuracy) versus 2.24 for ST (approximately 90.4% accuracy), replicating the consistent finding that foil rejection is more accurate than target recognition..
Two-way Interactions: Picture Type Modulation of Test Position Effects. The Test Position × picture Type interaction showed sharply diverging patterns for ST and TO pictures. The linear interaction was large and positive, b = 76.70, SE = 2.84, z = 27.02, p < .001, 95% CI [71.13, 82.26], indicating opposite linear trends—foil rejection declined (b = -40.96) while target recognition increased (b = 35.74). The quadratic interaction was also significant and negative, b = -27.92, SE = 3.26, z = -8.57, p < .001, 95% CI [-34.31, -21.54], indicating opposite curvatures for ST and TO pictures, with relatively higher performance at early and late positions. 
Average Performance Across Test Positions. To assess performance changes across test positions, we ran two analyses. Averaging across picture types on the logit scale showed no significant overall trends, linear: b = -2.61, SE = 2.48, 95% CI [-7.47, 2.25]; quadratic: b = -4.91, SE = 3.02, 95% CI [-10.84, 1.01]. These null effects reflected opposing picture-type trends (TO: b = -40.96; ST: b = 35.74).
When picture types were collapsed, overall accuracy rose from 88.3% (positions 1–5) to 90.2% (9–12) and plateaued near 89.8% (16–20). The model showed a positive linear trend, b = 10.19, SE = 2.79, z = 3.66, p < .001, and a negative quadratic trend, b = -11.42, SE = 2.19, z = -5.22, p < .001, indicating an inverted U-shaped pattern.
Thus, overall accuracy improved modestly (~2%), but this gain reflected opposing effects: ST improved while TO declined.
## Initial Between-List Effects
 Across lists we see clear evidence of output interference, as shown in Figure 8.a. Overall performance drops due to a strong decrease in Hs (green). CRs (orange) are largely stable over lists. 
Overall Patterns Across Lists. For TO pictures, the linear trend was not significant, b = 0.54, SE = 2.61, z = 0.21, p = .835, 95% CI [-4.57, 5.66], indicating that correct rejection rates remained stable across lists with no systematic increase or decrease. A significant positive quadratic trend emerged (b = 11.38, SE = 2.29, z = 4.97, p < .001, 95% CI [6.89, 15.86]), indicating a reliable non-linear deviation across lists. Visual inspection (Figure 8) suggests that this deviation reflects a shallow mid-list dip rather than a pronounced inverted-U pattern. In other words, TO accuracy was relatively stable for early and late lists, with a modest reduction in the middle. The positive sign of the quadratic term could arise from orthogonal contrast coding and might not be interpreted as implying an upward (U-shaped) curvature in the raw data.
Picture type effects. ST pictures showed substantially lower overall recognition accuracy than TO pictures, b = -0.86, SE = 0.04, z = -23.30, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.93, -0.78]. Across all lists, TO pictures averaged 93.6% correct rejection (range: 92.2%–94.2%), while ST pictures averaged 86.2% correct recognition (range: 82.6%–94.2%), demonstrating considerably better foil rejection than target recognition.  
Two-way interactions: picture type modulation of list effects. The strong negative interaction between List Number and picture Type, b = -55.06, SE = 2.50, z = -22.06, p < .001, 95% CI [-59.95, -50.17], shows that ST recognition significantly decreased from early to late lists (94.2% in list 1 to 82.6% in list 7), while TO recognition remained stable (92.2%–94.2% across lists).

The quadratic interaction, b = 32.66, SE = 2.38, z = 13.74, p < .001, 95% CI [28.00, 37.32], revealed a significant difference in the inverted U-shaped pattern between picture types. TO pictures showed a modest inverted U (b = 11.38), while ST exhibited a more pronounced curvilinear pattern (b = 44.04), indicating stronger primacy and recency effects for ST pictures. 
## Final Within-List Study Position Effects 
Figure 9.1.a.b: shows that study position shows little effect of initial study position. This result is notably different from the serial study position effects observed during study of the initial lists. This result suggests that the initial list study position effects were not due to changes in trace strength, or they would have appeared in final testing. Forgetting did take place between initial lists and final testing, but it is hard to imagine forgetting that would only affect the primacy and recency portions of the study lists.
Overall patterns by study position. Across pictures, the linear trend for SO items was modest and did not reach significance, b = −5.56, SE = 3.02, z = −1.84, p = .065, 95% CI [−11.47, 0.35], indicating only a slight tendency for accuracy to dip at later study positions. A reliable positive quadratic component remained, b = 10.31, SE = 2.80, z = 3.68, p < .001, 95% CI [4.82, 15.80], reflecting the familiar primacy–recency bend.
Picture type effects. ST pictures showed much higher recognition accuracy than SO pictures, b = 1.43, SE = 0.03, z = 42.92, p < .001, 95% CI [1.37, 1.50]. This large difference indicates that items previously tested were remembered substantially better than those only studied, reflecting the additional exposure and retrieval opportunities for ST items.  The estimated marginal means were 0.7641 logits (≈68.22% accuracy) for SO and 2.1979 logits (≈90.01% accuracy) for ST, underscoring a robust testing effect.
Two-way interactions: picture type modulation of study position effects. The Study Position Linear × picture Type interaction remained nonsignificant, b = −1.89, SE = 5.50, z = −0.34, p = .730, 95% CI [−12.67, 8.88], indicating similar linear trends for SO (b = −5.56) and ST (b = −7.46) items. Both declined similarly across study positions, suggesting no differential protection from retrieval practice.  

## Final Within-List Test Position Effects
Final test accuracy strongly increased with initial list test position for both targets and foils, suggesting initial list test position effects were due to trace strength differences. The increase in foils was opposite the initial list decrease. There is no simple way to explain these test position effects. The increase for both initial foils and initial targets suggests some sort of distortion at the start of testing that dissipates as testing continues. 
Overall patterns by test position. Across pictures, a strong positive linear trend persisted, b = 22.41, SE = 2.90, z = 7.73, p < .001, 95% CI [16.73, 28.10], indicating that recognition continued to improve for items tested later within the initial list. A negative quadratic component, b = −9.57, SE = 2.98, z = −3.21, p = .0013, 95% CI [−15.42, −3.73], captured the slowing of this improvement toward the end of the list. 
Picture type effects. Only ST and TO pictures have initial test positions and are included in this analysis. TO pictures showed substantially lower recognition accuracy than ST pictures, b = -1.51, SE = 0.03, z = -45.70, p < .001, 95% CI [-1.58, -1.45], with observed means of 64.2% versus 88.2%. This large effect demonstrates that full initial exposure (study plus test) provides substantially greater benefit than partial exposure (test only). 
Two-way interactions: picture type modulation of test position effects. The Linear × Picture Type interaction was nonsignificant, b = 4.43, SE = 3.71, z = 1.19, p = .233, 95% CI [−2.85, 11.71], indicating that TO pictures (slope = 26.84) and ST pictures (slope = 22.41) climbed at comparable rates. The quadratic interaction was also nonsignificant, b = −3.45, SE = 3.76, z = −0.92, p = .359, 95% CI [−10.82, 3.92], indicating that both picture types displayed similar curvature (ST quadratic = −9.57; TO quadratic = −13.03), with accuracy leveling off modestly in later test positions.
## Final Between-List Effects by Final Test Order and Initial List Order
Note that the backward and forward results are mirrors of each other measured by input list position or final test position. Thus in Figure 10, the first and second columns of row 1 show the same data reversed, and the first and second columns of row 2 show the same data. 
Figure 10.1.a shows output interference (OI) for all picture types. The final test order manipulation (forward, backward, random) showed effects important for modeling. A] Initial targets have the highest final Hs, suggesting they were stored strongly; there is little OI for these pictures, possibly due to ceiling effects. B] Pictures studied only and pictures tested only had much lower Hs and differed little from each other suggesting they had been stored much more weakly than targets (i.e studied and tested pictures) but were stored with similar strengths. C] The backward condition showed strong recency, and strong OI compared to the other conditions. It is assumed that this is due to two factors acting in concert: 1] use of enough changing context in final test retrieval to produce recency – better performance for the most recent lists seen; 2] output interference (OI) that causes performance to decrease as testing continues (see the modeling section for a discussion of the factors causing OI). D] the backward condition also showed some primacy. The best account is a gradual reconstruction of changing context as the testing moves to earlier lists. This idea will be used to explain the forward data. E] The forward condition results show greatly reduced performance as lists continue. Our best account is reconstruction of changing context. E.g. When the participants are told that the targets are coming from the first list, they manage to reconstruct context that is more similar to that stored originally than the current context. This better context continues to be used as testing continues. F] The random condition provides feedback that tells the participant what list a target came from, but that list randomly jumps among lists as testing continues. It is unclear under these circumstances whether any reconstruction of context would occur. Thus in the random condition it is assumed that the context used at the start of final testing is that after the change from initial lists to final testing. (Looking ahead to the modeling, it should be mentioned that an account for the large output interference observed, as seen most unambiguously in the random condition, requires changes in context regularly as final testing proceeds). 
 

Item type effects. Different item types showed substantially different overall recognition accuracy compared to FTO (the reference). ST items were markedly more accurate than FTO, b = 0.45, SE = 0.02, z = 18.04, p < .001, 95% CI [0.40, 0.50], with an estimated marginal mean of 2.08 logits (≈88.93% accuracy). In contrast, SO items (b = −0.95, SE = 0.02, z = −50.70, p < .001, 95% CI [−0.99, −0.91], marginal mean = 0.69 logits, ≈66.51%) and TO items (b = −1.07, SE = 0.02, z = −57.64, p < .001, 95% CI [−1.11, −1.03], marginal mean = 0.57 logits, ≈63.79%) performed worse than FTO (marginal mean = 1.64 logits, ≈83.69%).
Condition effects. Overall accuracy still did not differ by condition: backward vs. forward, b = 0.05, SE = 0.10, z = 0.52, p = .601, 95% CI [−0.14, 0.25]; random vs. forward, b = 0.03, SE = 0.09, z = 0.33, p = .739, 95% CI [−0.14, 0.19], confirming that presentation order modulated list-position patterns but not baseline performance.
Ordering type effects. The main effect of ordering type remained nonsignificant, b = −0.004, SE = 0.018, z = −0.25, p = .800, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.03], indicating no baseline accuracy difference between by final-test order and by initial-list order perspectives.
Linear trends by list order. For the forward condition with FTO items (reference), the linear trend was b = −38.08, SE = 6.94, z = −5.49, p < .001, 95% CI [−51.67, −24.49], indicating robust output interference. Backward testing produced a far steeper decline (interaction adds −37.51, yielding b ≈ −75.59), while the random condition’s decline was intermediate (interaction adds −10.81, b ≈ −48.89). When list order was expressed by initial position, slopes reversed strongly (interaction b = 65.46, p < .001), underscoring the divergence between test order and original study order.

Because the forward and backward groups share perfectly correlated orders (identical or mirrored), the interaction involving list-order perspective primarily reflects the independent contrast available in the random condition, where initial and final orders are uncorrelated.

Backward testing produced a much steeper decline than forward testing, b = −37.51, SE = 8.99, z = −4.17, p < .001, 95% CI [−55.14, −19.88], yielding a combined slope of approximately −75.59. The random condition showed a weaker (and nonsignificant) difference from forward testing, b = −10.81, SE = 7.70, z = −1.40, p = .161, giving a combined slope of roughly −48.89.
Two-way interactions: Linear × Item Type. Slopes differed sharply by item type. Relative to FTO items (slope = −38.08), ST pictures declined much more steeply, b = −31.76, SE = 9.10, z = −3.49, p < .001, 95% CI [−49.58, −13.93], for a combined slope near −69.83. TO items were somewhat steeper (b = −8.30, SE = 6.56, z = −1.27, p = .206; slope ≈ −46.38), whereas SO items declined more gently (b = 3.85, SE = 6.63, z = 0.58, p = .561; slope ≈ −34.23).

ST items: b = 52.35, SE = 8.91, z = 5.87, p < .001, 95% CI [34.88, 69.82], combined quadratic ≈ 60.47. These effects signal that items with study or test exposure showed pronounced curvature relative to final-test-only foils.
 


# Results of Experiment 2
## Data Exclusion and Quality Control
A total of 199 participants completed the experiment through online recruitment via Prolific. Data quality screening was conducted using multiple exclusion criteria following standard practices in cognitive psychology research (Van Selst & Jolicoeur, 1994). At the participant level, individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) below-chance performance (accuracy < 50%), indicating random responding; (b) overall task accuracy more than 2.5 standard deviations below the sample mean; (c) median response time more than 3 standard deviations from the sample mean, indicating atypical response patterns; or (d) more than 10% of responses faster than 200 ms, indicating insufficient processing time or rushed responding. At the trial level, responses faster than 150 ms or slower than 3,500 ms were excluded from all analyses, consistent with established response window parameters.
Thirteen participants (6.5%) were excluded based on participant-level criteria. Specifically, nine participants exhibited below-chance performance (M = 0.48, SD = 0.01, range = 0.48–0.50), indicating they were responding randomly or not engaging meaningfully with the task. Two additional participants exhibited extremely low accuracy (22.9% and 29.1%, both >3 SD below the mean) combined with below-chance performance. Three participants were excluded due to extreme response time outliers (median RTs of 281 ms, 1,654 ms, and 1,669 ms; |z| > 3.3), with one also exhibiting rushed responding (19.5% of responses < 200 ms). At the trial level, 479 trials (0.31% of all test trials) were excluded due to response times outside the 150–3,500 ms window (355 trials < 150 ms, 124 trials > 3,500 ms).
The final sample consisted of 186 participants contributing 156,214 valid test trials. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated for their time in accordance with institutional review board guidelines.

This analysis tested whether serial position effects during encoding influenced immediate recognition performance for studied targets.

Test position effects during initial testing were examined to assess whether output interference accumulated during the test phase and how confusing foils affected performance. A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, test position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts and slopes for test position by participant.

A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, list number (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant.

A GLMM was fitted with accuracy as the dependent variable, study position (linear and quadratic terms) and item type as fixed effects, and random intercepts by participant.

## Initial Within-List Test Position Effects 
Figure 12.1.b shows test position effects conditioned on the various types of test pictures. The solid lines show current list target Hs and current list foil CRs. These show roughly the pattern of Experiment 1, with Hs rising and CRs falling as tests continue. The explanation for these results from both studies will be presented in the modeling presentation.
The most interesting results are in the dashed lines that show correct rejections (CRs) for current list foils that had been seen on the prior trial (termed ‘confusing foils’). Due to their presence on the prior trial these test pictures are highly familiar and as a result are given many false alarms (FAs), or equivalently are given low CRs. The most familiar pictures are those studied and tested on the prior trial (as demonstrated by final test results to be shown shortly). If familiarity (REM odds, or some other measure of degree of familiarity) were used to respond, these pictures would be given the greatest false alarms (FAs), and equivalently the lowest CRs. Yet these very familiar pictures produce CRs higher than pictures from the prior trial that were studied only, and not much lower than CRs for foils seen on the prior trial. Such results suggest that participants faced with highly familiar pictures that are not from the current list start using a recall process: They recall a trace likely to be one produced by the test picture and assess the trace contents. When they find information in that trace that identifies the origin of the picture producing the trace to be the prior list, they ignore the high familiarity and respond new. This is a process known as ‘recall to reject’ (e.g. Rotello, Macmillan & Van Tassel, 2000). This process is described in detail in the modeling section.
